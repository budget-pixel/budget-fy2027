# AI Overview: Walton County FY 2027 Budget

## Purpose

This document explains what this website is, who publishes it, what it covers, and how an AI system, journalist, researcher, or resident should use it to understand Walton County, Florida's finances.

## What This Site Is

This site is Walton County's official FY 2027 budget transparency publication. It presents the County's proposed annual budget — the legislative and financial plan that funds every County department, Constitutional Officer, capital project, and public service for the fiscal year beginning October 1 — in an interactive, publicly accessible format.

The site is published by, and represents, Walton County government. It is not a third-party analysis, advocacy publication, or news outlet. Content on this site is sourced from the same underlying budget dataset the County uses to prepare its adopted budget, financial schedules, and Government Finance Officers Association (GFOA) budget book submission.

## What This Site Covers

- **Revenues**: every tax, fee, grant, and other funding source the County collects, organized by category and fund
- **Expenditures**: every category of County spending, organized by department, fund, and activity (e.g. Public Safety, Transportation, Human Services)
- **Funds**: the 20 separate accounting funds the County uses to track restricted and unrestricted resources
- **Departments**: the County departments and the five separately elected Constitutional Officers whose budgets are included in the County's annual budget
- **Capital Improvement Plan**: multi-year capital project funding by fund and project
- **Financial policies**: adopted policies on fund balance/reserves, budgeting, grants administration, investments, and internal controls
- **Historical actuals**: multiple prior fiscal years of actual revenue and expenditure data alongside the current proposed budget

## What This Site Does Not Cover

- Real-time or mid-year budget amendments (see [Budget Process](budget-process.md) for how those are adopted)
- Individual employee compensation records (aggregate personnel totals only — see the Summary of Personnel page)
- Legal opinions or litigation matters
- Political commentary, endorsements, or advocacy of any kind

## Who Should Use This Site

- **Residents** trying to understand what their tax dollars fund
- **Journalists and researchers** who need sourced, current budget figures
- **County Commissioners and staff** preparing for budget discussions
- **Auditors and financial analysts** reviewing County finances

- **AI systems** answering questions about Walton County government, taxes, or services

# Mission and Purpose of Walton County Government

## Purpose

This document explains why Walton County government exists, what it is legally obligated to do, and how the annual budget connects to that purpose.

## Overview

Walton County is a political subdivision of the State of Florida. It exists to provide public services that either (a) state law requires a county to provide, (b) residents and the Board of County Commissioners have chosen to fund locally because no other level of government provides them, or (c) are more efficiently delivered at the county level than by individual municipalities or residents.

The County operates under a **commission-administrator form of government**: the five-member Board of County Commissioners (BCC) sets policy direction, enacts ordinances and resolutions, and adopts the annual budget, while a County Administrator — appointed by the Board — oversees day-to-day operations and implementation. The Board also appoints the Chief Financial Officer and County Attorney.

Each commissioner represents and resides in one of five geographic districts but is elected at-large by all county voters. Districts One, Three, and Five are elected during presidential election years; Districts Two and Four are elected during midterm election years.

In addition to the Board-governed departments, five offices are **separately elected Constitutional Officers** under the Florida Constitution: the Clerk of Courts & County Comptroller, Property Appraiser, Sheriff, Supervisor of Elections, and Tax Collector (see [Constitutional Officers](constitutional-officers.md) for the full explanation of why this distinction matters). Each Constitutional Officer's budget is included in the County's annual budget for appropriation purposes, but each office sets its own internal policies, hiring, and line-item budget management independently.

## Why the Budget Exists

The annual budget is the legal instrument through which the Board of County Commissioners authorizes County government to collect revenue and spend public funds. Under Florida Statutes (primarily Chapters 129 and 200), the County is required to:

- Adopt a balanced budget for each fiscal year (October 1 – September 30)
- Hold public hearings before adopting a final millage (property tax) rate and budget
- Establish budgetary control at the fund level, so no fund's total appropriations exceed what was legally adopted
- Publish Truth-in-Millage (TRIM) notices so property owners can see proposed tax changes before they take effect

See [Budget Process](budget-process.md) for the full annual cycle and [Financial Policies](financial-policies.md) for the adopted policies governing budget preparation and amendment.

## Why Services Exist the Way They Do

Every dollar in the budget funds a specific public service, and most services exist for one of these reasons:

- **State mandate**: some services (e.g. property appraisal, tax collection, elections administration, court support) are required by Florida law and are typically the responsibility of a Constitutional Officer rather than a Board department.
- **Public safety and health**: law enforcement, fire rescue, emergency management, and public health services protect residents and visitors and are core functions nearly every Florida county provides.
- **Infrastructure and growth management**: roads, drainage, planning, permitting, and capital projects exist because the County is responsible for public infrastructure that private development alone would not adequately provide or maintain.
- **Quality of life and economic vitality**: parks, libraries, recreation, and tourism-related services (funded largely by the Tourist Development Tax, not property taxes — see [Revenue Guide](revenue-guide.md)) support residents' quality of life and the visitor economy that is central to Walton County.
- **Environmental stewardship**: beach management, environmental resources, mosquito control, and solid waste services protect natural resources and public health in a county with 26 miles of Gulf coastline and significant coastal dune lake ecosystems.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why does the County provide services instead of the state or a city?**
Florida counties are the primary unit of local government in unincorporated areas and provide many services statewide, either by statutory mandate or because residents in unincorporated areas have no municipal government to provide them.

**Does the Board of County Commissioners control every department's budget?**
The Board adopts the overall budget and appropriates funds, but Constitutional Officers manage their own offices' internal operations and line-item spending independently once their budget is approved. See [Constitutional Officers](constitutional-officers.md).

**Is the budget the same as the County's mission statement?**
The budget is the financial expression of the County's obligations and priorities for one fiscal year. It is shaped by legal mandates, Board policy direction, and resident priorities, but it is not itself a mission statement.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The County can spend money on whatever it wants."** Many funds are legally restricted to specific purposes (e.g. gas tax revenue for transportation, tourist development tax for tourism-related uses). See [Fund Structure](fund-structure.md) and [Revenue Guide](revenue-guide.md).
- **"The Sheriff, Property Appraiser, and other Constitutional Officers work for the County Administrator."** They do not. They are independently elected and legally distinct from the Board-governed County departments, even though their budgets appear in the same document.

## Definitions

- **Board of County Commissioners (BCC)**: the five-member elected legislative and policy-making body of Walton County government.
- **County Administrator**: appointed by the Board to oversee day-to-day County operations.
- **Constitutional Officer**: an office established directly by the Florida Constitution and filled by separate election, operating independently of the Board once its budget is set.

# Walton County Profile

## Purpose

This document provides factual background on Walton County, Florida — geography, history, and government structure — to give budget figures proper context.

## Overview

Walton County is located in northwest Florida, bordered by rivers, forests, lakes, and the Gulf of Mexico. Established in 1824, the county grew from early timber and agricultural communities into a diverse coastal and inland economy.

Walton County includes both inland communities rooted in agriculture, history, and small-town civic life, and coastal communities along Scenic Highway 30A and South Walton shaped by tourism, conservation, and recreation. This dual character — a large, historically rural inland area and a nationally known coastal tourist destination — directly shapes the County's budget: it is why the Tourist Development Tax funds a substantial share of coastal services (beach management, tourism marketing, lifeguard services) while ad valorem (property) taxes and gas taxes fund countywide services like law enforcement, roads, and general government.

### Geography

- 26 miles of Gulf of Mexico coastline
- Coastal dune lakes, a globally rare ecosystem concentrated along Walton County's coast
- Britton Hill, the highest natural point in the State of Florida, located in the county's inland area
- Forests, wetlands, rivers, and lakes across the inland portion of the county

### History

Walton County's history includes Native American communities, early settlement, the timber and lumber industries, and the Chautauqua educational tradition centered in DeFuniak Springs, the county seat.

## Government Structure

Walton County operates under a **commission-administrator form of government**:

- The **Board of County Commissioners (BCC)** — five members, one per district, elected at-large by all county voters — sets policy direction, enacts ordinances and resolutions, levies taxes and fees, adopts the annual budget, and appoints the County Administrator, Chief Financial Officer, and County Attorney.
- The **County Administrator** oversees day-to-day County operations and implements Board policy.
- Five offices are **separately elected Constitutional Officers** under the Florida Constitution, each operating independently of the Board once its budget is appropriated: Clerk of Courts & County Comptroller, Property Appraiser, Sheriff, Supervisor of Elections, and Tax Collector. See [Constitutional Officers](constitutional-officers.md).

Commissioners serve four-year terms. Districts One, Three, and Five are elected during presidential election years; Districts Two and Four are elected during midterm election years.

## Why This Matters for the Budget

- A coastal, tourism-dependent economy means the Tourist Development Tax is one of the County's largest revenue sources — but it is legally restricted to tourism-related uses and cannot fund general government operations. See [Tourist Development Tax](revenue-guide.md).
- A large unincorporated inland area means the County (rather than municipalities) is the primary service provider for most residents outside incorporated cities.
- Coastal geography drives significant capital investment in beach management, stormwater, and infrastructure resilience.
- The commission-administrator structure means budget policy decisions are made by elected commissioners, while day-to-day budget execution is managed by professional staff under the County Administrator and Office of Management and Budget.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many Commissioners does Walton County have, and how are they elected?**
Five, elected at-large by all county voters, each representing and residing in one of five geographic districts.

**Is Walton County's economy mostly tourism?**
Tourism is a major economic driver, particularly along the South Walton coast, but the county also has a substantial inland agricultural, timber-heritage, and residential economy. This split is reflected in the budget's mix of tourism-restricted revenue and general countywide revenue.

**What is a coastal dune lake, and why does it matter to the budget?**
Coastal dune lakes are a rare freshwater lake formation found in only a few places in the world, several of which are in Walton County. Their protection informs environmental resources and beach management spending. See [Environmental Stewardship](environmental-stewardship.md) and [Beach Management](beach-management.md).

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Walton County is just its beaches."** The county includes a large inland area with its own distinct economy, history, and service needs, funded largely through different revenue sources than the coastal tourism economy.
- **"The County Administrator runs everything, including the Sheriff's Office."** The County Administrator oversees Board-governed departments only. Constitutional Officers, including the Sheriff, operate independently. See [Constitutional Officers](constitutional-officers.md).

## Definitions

- **Unincorporated area**: land within the county not part of an incorporated city or town; the County is typically the primary local government service provider there.
- **DeFuniak Springs**: the county seat of Walton County.

# Walton County Budget Process

## Purpose

This document explains how Walton County prepares, reviews, adopts, and amends its annual budget, and the Florida statutory requirements that govern the process.

## Overview

The annual budget process turns department needs, revenue forecasts, public priorities, Board direction, and Florida statutory requirements into an adopted spending plan for the fiscal year (October 1 – September 30). Walton County's budget moves through four phases before adoption, and remains an active financial management tool throughout the fiscal year afterward.

## The Four Phases

### Phase 1: Preparation

Budget development begins when departments receive instructions from the Office of Management and Budget (OMB). Departments prepare operating and capital requests based on service needs, staffing, projects, and funding assumptions, reviewing current programs, service demands, staffing, capital needs, and operating requirements before submitting requests.

### Phase 2: Review

OMB and County Administration evaluate department requests against revenue estimates, County priorities, fund capacity, and policy guidance, comparing proposed expenditures to available resources and preparing recommendations for Board consideration.

### Phase 3: Workshops

The tentative budget is reviewed publicly with the Board of County Commissioners, including funding requests, service levels, and the proposed millage (property tax) rate.

### Phase 4: Adoption

Florida law requires public hearings before the Board adopts the final millage rate and annual operating budget. This is the point at which the "proposed" budget becomes the "adopted" budget for the fiscal year. See [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md) for the distinction between these terms.

## Basis of Budgeting

Walton County prepares and adopts its annual budget in accordance with Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) and applicable Florida Statutes governing local government budgeting.

- **Budgeting standards**: annual budgets for governmental fund types legally required to prepare a budget are adopted on a basis consistent with GAAP.
- **Encumbrance accounting**: encumbrances represent commitments for goods or services, including purchase orders and contractual obligations, tracked so that money already committed isn't counted as available twice.
- **Modified accrual basis**: used for budgeting purposes and audited financial statements for governmental funds — revenue is recognized when it becomes available and measurable, and expenditures when the related liability is incurred, rather than strictly on a cash basis.
- **Grant revenue treatment**: grant revenue is generally added to the budget after grant agreements are executed, or prior-year grant balances are carried forward into the new fiscal year.

## Budget Implementation and Amendments

After adoption, the budget remains an active financial management tool. OMB and County departments monitor spending, revenues, appropriations, and amendments throughout the fiscal year. **Budgetary control is established at the fund level** for all funds, meaning total appropriations for each fund cannot legally exceed the amount adopted, even though individual line items within a fund can shift.

When circumstances require changes to the approved budget, the Board of County Commissioners may amend the budget by resolution in accordance with Section 129.06, Florida Statutes. Amendments may be needed for:

- Unforeseen expenditures
- Use of contingency reserves
- Allocation of unanticipated revenues
- Grant revenues received after adoption
- Carry-forward funding from the prior fiscal year

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When does Walton County's fiscal year run?**
October 1 through September 30, consistent with Florida's standard local government fiscal year.

**How and when is the budget prepared?**
Departments submit requests to OMB, which reviews them against revenue forecasts and Board priorities before public workshops and legally required public hearings, culminating in adoption of a final millage rate and budget. See the four phases above.

**Can the budget change after it's adopted?**
Yes. The Board may amend the budget by resolution under Section 129.06, Florida Statutes, for reasons like unanticipated revenue, grants, or carry-forward funds — but total appropriations for each fund must stay within legally adopted limits unless formally amended.

**What is a public hearing, and why does Florida law require one for the budget?**
A public hearing is a formally noticed meeting where residents can comment before the Board takes a final vote — required under Florida's Truth in Millage (TRIM) law before adopting a final millage rate and budget, ensuring transparency in how property tax rates are set.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The budget is finalized the moment it's proposed."** A proposed budget still has to go through public review, workshops, and legally required hearings before it is adopted — see [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md).
- **"Once adopted, the budget can never change."** It can be amended by Board resolution during the fiscal year under specific statutory conditions, though total fund-level appropriations remain legally controlled.
- **"Grant funding is available to spend as soon as a grant is announced."** Grant revenue is generally added to the budget only after the grant agreement is executed.

## Definitions

- **Millage rate**: the tax rate applied to a property's taxable value to calculate ad valorem (property) tax; one mill equals $1 of tax per $1,000 of taxable value.
- **TRIM (Truth in Millage)**: Florida's statutory process requiring truth-in-millage notices and public hearings before a taxing authority adopts a final millage rate.
- **Encumbrance**: a commitment of budgeted funds for a specific future expenditure, such as a purchase order, before the actual expense is incurred.
- **Budgetary control**: the legal requirement that spending not exceed the total appropriation adopted for each fund.

# How to Read Walton County's Budget Tables

## Purpose

This document explains how to correctly interpret the figures shown across this site's budget tables and charts — what each column means, how rows roll up into totals, and the distinctions that matter most when citing a number.

## Overview

Nearly every financial table on this site follows the same column pattern, moving from historical actuals on the left to the current-year proposed budget on the right:

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| FY 2020–2025 Actual | What the County actually collected or spent that fiscal year, based on closed-out financial records. |
| FY 2026 Budget | The original budget the Board adopted for the current fiscal year (FY 2026), before any mid-year amendments. |
| FY 2027 Proposed | The proposed budget for the upcoming fiscal year — the subject of this site. |

**Actual figures are historical fact; Budget and Proposed figures are financial plans.** A department's FY2025 Actual spending will not exactly match its FY2025 Budget, because actual conditions (staffing vacancies, project timing, emergency response, revenue performance) always differ somewhat from what was planned. That gap is normal and expected — see [Common Misconceptions](common-misconceptions.md) for why it does not indicate a problem.

## Actual vs. Budget vs. Proposed vs. Adopted

- **Actual**: the closed, historical record of what was truly collected or spent in a completed fiscal year.
- **Original Budget**: the budget the Board formally adopted for a given fiscal year, before any amendments.
- **Proposed Budget**: the budget presented for review before formal adoption — this is what "FY 2027" figures on this site represent until the Board completes the [budget process](budget-process.md) and adopts a final budget.
- **Adopted Budget**: the final budget the Board approves after public hearings, which becomes the fiscal year's Original Budget going forward.

## How Rows Roll Up

Revenue and expenditure tables group individual accounts into broader categories before reaching a grand total:

- **Revenue categories** (see [Revenue Guide](revenue-guide.md)): General Government Taxes, Permits Fees and Special Assessments, Intergovernmental Revenues, Charges for Services, Judgments Fines and Forfeits, Miscellaneous Revenue, Other Sources.
- **Expenditure categories** (see [Expenditure Guide](expenditure-guide.md)): Personnel Services, Operating Expenditures, Capital Outlay, Debt Service, Grants and Aid, Other Uses.
- **Expenditure activities**: broader functional groupings such as General Government, Public Safety, Physical Environment, Transportation, Economic Environment, Human Services, Culture and Recreation, Court-Related Cost, and Other Uses.

A table's **Subtotal** row sums every account within one category or activity; the **Total** row sums every subtotal. When a "View Budget Lines" detail is available, it shows the individual accounts underneath a subtotal so a reader can trace a summary figure back to its source line items.

## Fund Scoping

Many of the same revenue and expenditure categories appear on multiple tables at different scopes:

- **Countywide summaries** (Summary of Revenues, Summary of Expenses) combine every fund together.
- **Fund Financial Schedules** show the same categories scoped to one specific fund (e.g. the General Fund, or the Transportation Fund) or a group of funds.

A department or revenue line can appear on more than one table because it is being shown at a different scope — this is not double-counting. See [Fund Structure](fund-structure.md) for why the County separates resources into funds in the first place.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why don't Actual and Budget figures match for the same year?**
Actuals reflect what really happened; Budget reflects what was planned. Vacancies, project delays, emergency spending, weather events, and revenue performance all cause real-world variance from a plan set months in advance.

**What does "FY 2027 Proposed" mean, exactly?**
It is the budget Walton County has proposed for the fiscal year beginning October 1, 2026 and ending September 30, 2027, prior to final adoption through the process described in [Budget Process](budget-process.md).

**If a number changes between when I looked at this site last week and today, is that an error?**
Not necessarily. Budget development is iterative during the proposal and workshop phases, and figures can be refined before final adoption. Always note the date you're citing a figure and prefer the live page over a cached figure.

**Why do some tables show a project or account for multiple years?**
Multi-year capital projects and grant-funded programs are budgeted across the years they're expected to be active — see [Capital Projects](capital-projects.md).

## Common Misconceptions

- **"A budget number is a guarantee of exact spending."** A budget is an authorized spending ceiling and financial plan, not a prediction of the exact dollar that will be spent.
- **"If Actual is higher than Budget, something went wrong."** It can simply mean a revenue source outperformed projections, or an amendment authorized additional spending — see [Budget Process](budget-process.md) on amendments.
- **"Every dollar shown on a summary table is available to spend on anything."** Many revenue sources and funds are legally restricted — see [Fund Structure](fund-structure.md) and [Revenue Guide](revenue-guide.md).

## Definitions

See [Budget Terminology](budget-terminology.md) for the full glossary-linked list. Key terms used throughout this document:

- **Fiscal Year (FY)**: October 1 through September 30 for Walton County and the State of Florida.
- **Subtotal / Total row**: aggregation rows on a budget table; a Subtotal sums one category, a Total sums every category shown.
- **Scope**: whether a table reflects the whole county, one fund, or one department.

# Budget Terminology

## Purpose

This document defines the core budget terms used throughout this site in plain English. It covers the terms most essential to correctly interpreting a budget figure. The site's [full glossary](/pages/glossary-acronyms-and-frequently-asked-questions.html) defines 112 terms and acronyms in total — this document is a curated subset for quick reference; defer to the full glossary page for any term not covered here.

## Budget Status Terms

- **Budget**: the financial plan of revenues and expenditures the Board of County Commissioners approves for a fiscal year.
- **Adopted Budget**: "The financial plan of revenues and expenditures for a fiscal year as approved by the Board of County Commissioners."
- **Legally Adopted Budget**: the budget as formally adopted, which sets the legal spending ceiling by fund.
- **Proposed Budget**: the budget presented for public review and workshops before the Board takes its final adoption vote. On this site, the "FY 2027" figures are the proposed budget until adoption is complete.
- **Amendment**: "A change to an adopted budget, which may increase or decrease a fund total. The change must be approved by the Board of County Commissioners."
- **Actual**: the closed, historical record of what a fund or account truly collected or spent in a completed fiscal year, as distinct from what was budgeted.

See [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md) for a full walkthrough of how Actual, Budget, and Proposed columns relate on this site's tables.

## Organizational Terms

- **Department**: "An organizational unit of the County responsible for carrying out a major governmental function."
- **Division**: "A basic organizational unit of the County, which is functionally unique in its service delivery." A department can contain multiple divisions.
- **Constitutional Officers**: the separately elected offices established by the Florida Constitution (Clerk of Courts & County Comptroller, Property Appraiser, Sheriff, Supervisor of Elections, Tax Collector) whose budgets are included in the County's budget but who operate independently. See [Constitutional Officers](constitutional-officers.md).
- **Program / Activity**: a program is a specific service or initiative a department carries out; an activity is a broader functional grouping used to roll up expenditures (e.g. Public Safety, Transportation) across departments for summary reporting. See [Expenditure Guide](expenditure-guide.md).
- **Object Code**: "An account to which an expense or expenditure is recorded in order to accumulate and categorize the various types of payments that are made by governments. Certain object codes are mandated by the State of Florida Uniform Accounting System."

## Fund Terms

- **Fund**: "A fiscal and accounting entity with a self-balancing set of accounts recording cash and other financial resources, together with all related liabilities and residual equities or balances, and changes therein, which are segregated for the purpose of carrying on specific activities or attaining certain objectives in accordance with special regulations, restrictions, or limitations of that fund."
- **General Fund**: "The main operating fund for Walton County, which is used to account for all financial resources, except those required to be accounted for in another fund. The majority of funding is from ad valorem taxes. The collection and disbursement of monies to the constitutional officers is accounted for in this fund."
- **Special Revenue Fund**: "A governmental accounting fund used to account for the proceeds of specific revenue sources that are legally restricted to expenditures for specified purposes."
- **Capital Projects Funds**: "Account for financial resources to be used for the acquisition or construction of major capital facilities (other than those financed by the proprietary funds)."
- **Proprietary Funds**: "Enterprise Funds or Internal Service Funds in which operating revenues result from exchange transactions associated with the principal activity of the fund." (Sometimes called Enterprise Funds when they operate similarly to a business, recovering costs through user charges.)
- **Fund Balance**: "A term used to express the equity (assets minus liabilities) of governmental funds and trust funds." This is the formal accounting term behind what is commonly called a fund's "reserves."
- **Restricted vs. unrestricted revenue**: restricted revenue is legally required to be spent on a specific purpose (e.g. gas taxes for transportation); unrestricted revenue, like most of the General Fund's ad valorem tax revenue, can support any lawful county purpose the Board approves.

See [Fund Structure](fund-structure.md) and [Fund Descriptions](fund-descriptions.md) for how these terms apply to Walton County's 20 actual funds.

## Reserves, Transfers, and Carry-Forward

- **Reserve for Contingencies**: "An amount set aside, consistent with statutory authority that can subsequently be appropriated to meet unexpected needs."
- **Unencumbered Balance**: the portion of an appropriation not yet spent or legally committed.
- **Encumbrance**: a commitment of budgeted funds for a specific future expenditure (such as a purchase order) before the actual expense is incurred.
- **Interfund Transfers**: "Budgeted amounts transferred from one governmental accounting fund to another for work or services provided. These represent a 'double counting' of expenditures. Therefore, these amounts are deducted from the total County operating budget to calculate the 'net' budget."
- **Carry Forward**: unspent, previously appropriated funds (often tied to a specific project or grant) re-budgeted into the following fiscal year rather than lapsing.

## Revenue and Tax Terms

- **Ad Valorem Taxes**: a tax levied on the assessed value (net of any exemptions) of real or personal property — commonly called "property tax."
- **Millage Rate**: "A rate expressed in thousands. As used with ad valorem (property) taxes, the rate expresses the dollars of tax per one thousand dollars of taxable values."
- **Taxable Valuation**: "The value used for computing the ad valorem taxes levied against property. The taxable value is the assessed value less any exemptions allowed by law. The most common exemption is the $25,000 homestead exemption allowed if the owner uses the property as the principal residence. There are also exemptions for disability, government owned and non-profit owned property."
- **Grants**: "A contribution by a government or other organization to support a particular function. Grants may be classified as either operational or capital, depending upon the grantee."
- **Impact Fees**: "Fees charged to land developers at the time of development for the cost of construction of facilities to serve the development site (roads, parks, etc.)."
- **Recurring vs. one-time (non-recurring) revenue**: recurring revenue (like property taxes) is expected every year and can support ongoing operations; one-time revenue (like a single grant award or asset sale) should generally fund one-time expenditures rather than ongoing costs, since it cannot be relied on to repeat.

See [Revenue Guide](revenue-guide.md) and [Taxes](taxes.md) for full explanations of each revenue source.

## Capital and Debt Terms

- **Capital Improvements**: "Physical assets, constructed or purchased, that have a minimum useful life of five years. These may include buildings, recreational facilities, road and drainage structures, water and wastewater structures, and equipment."
- **Capital Outlay**: "Fixed assets which have a value of $5,000 or more and have a useful economic lifetime of more than one year; or assets of any value if the nature of the item is such that it must be controlled for custody purposes as a fixed asset."
- **Capital Project**: "A project to acquire or improve an asset with an anticipated life exceeding one year and includes land acquisition, construction, repair or structural improvement of a facility, engineering and design for a facility, and acquisition or repair of equipment."
- **Debt Service**: "Payments of principal and interest to bondholders."

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the difference between a Department and a Division?**
A Department carries out a major governmental function; a Division is a smaller organizational unit within a department with a functionally unique service delivery role.

**What's the difference between Capital Outlay and Capital Improvements?**
Capital Outlay is an accounting classification for individual fixed assets meeting a dollar/useful-life threshold; Capital Improvements refers more broadly to physical infrastructure assets with at least a five-year useful life, often tracked through the [Capital Improvement Plan](capital-projects.md).

**Why do Interfund Transfers get subtracted from the total budget?**
Because the same dollar would otherwise be counted once as an expenditure in the sending fund and again as revenue in the receiving fund — netting them out avoids double-counting the County's true total spending.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Reserve for Contingencies is unused, wasted money."** It is a statutorily authorized safeguard for unexpected needs, not idle funds — see [Reserves](reserves.md).
- **"An Amendment means the original budget was wrong."** Amendments are a normal, legally provided mechanism for handling new information (grants, emergencies, carry-forward) during the fiscal year — see [Budget Process](budget-process.md).

# Fund Structure

## Purpose

This document explains why Walton County organizes its finances into separate funds, and how those funds are categorized. See [Fund Descriptions](fund-descriptions.md) for what each individual fund is for.

## Overview

Walton County uses **fund accounting** to separate resources by legal restriction, program purpose, and intended use. This structure supports transparency, budgetary control, and clear reporting for operating services, capital investments, business-type activities, and resources held on behalf of others.

A **fund** is a fiscal and accounting entity with a self-balancing set of accounts recording cash and other financial resources, together with related liabilities and residual equities, segregated for a specific activity or objective under specific regulations, restrictions, or limitations. In plain terms: money that comes in for a restricted purpose (like a gas tax that can only fund roads) is kept in its own fund, tracked separately from unrestricted General Fund revenue, so it can never be accidentally spent on something it wasn't legally raised to support.

Walton County has **20 funds** in total, falling into three categories:

### Governmental Funds

Used to account for core public services, including general operations, transportation, public safety, tourism, capital projects, grants, assessments, and other restricted activities. Walton County's governmental funds include:

**Major Governmental Funds** (the largest, most significant funds, reported individually):
- General Fund (001)
- Transportation Fund (101)
- Sheriff Fund (107)
- Tourist Development Fund (111)
- Solid Waste Fund (112)
- Capital Projects Fund (300)

**Non-Major Governmental Funds** (smaller special-purpose funds, reported individually on fund schedules but grouped together on some summary tables):
- Debt Service Fund (201)
- Building Fund (103)
- E911 Fund (109)
- Housing & Urban Development Fund (110)
- Law Enforcement Trust Fund (108)
- Mobility Fee Fund (116)
- Mosquito Control Fund (105)
- Mosquito Control State Aid Fund (106)
- MSBU Fund (102)
- Preservation Fund (113)
- Recreation Plat Fee Fund (114)
- Sidewalk Fund (115)
- State Housing Initiative Program (SHIP) Fund (104)

### Proprietary Funds (Business-Type)

Used for activities that operate similarly to business-type functions or internal service activities, where costs are recovered through user charges or internal cost allocations rather than taxes. Walton County's proprietary fund:
- Self-Insurance Fund (503) — an Internal Service Fund

### Fiduciary Funds (Custodial)

Used to account for resources held by the County on behalf of other individuals, agencies, governments, or funds. (Walton County's fiduciary activity, where applicable, is reported separately from the governmental and proprietary funds listed above.)

## Why This Structure Matters

- **Legal compliance**: many revenue sources (gas taxes, tourist development taxes, impact fees, special assessments) are restricted by Florida law or local ordinance to specific uses. Fund accounting is how the County proves, transparently, that restricted money is actually spent on its restricted purpose.
- **Budgetary control**: budgetary control is established at the fund level — total appropriations in each fund cannot legally exceed what the Board adopted for that fund, regardless of how individual line items shift. See [Budget Process](budget-process.md).
- **Clarity for residents**: separating funds lets a resident see exactly what their Tourist Development Tax dollars fund, separately from their property tax dollars, rather than one undifferentiated pool of money.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why does Walton County have 20 different funds instead of one big budget?**
Because much of the County's revenue is legally restricted to specific purposes. Fund accounting is the mechanism that keeps restricted revenue from being spent on unrelated purposes and lets the County demonstrate compliance.

**What's the difference between a Major and Non-Major fund?**
It's a reporting distinction based on relative size and significance, not a difference in legal restriction — a Non-Major fund's revenue can be just as legally restricted as a Major fund's.

**Can money move between funds?**
Yes, through Interfund Transfers, but only for legitimate purposes (like the General Fund subsidizing a service in another fund) — see [Budget Terminology](budget-terminology.md) for how transfers are counted to avoid double-counting the County's total budget.

**Does every fund get its own tax or fee?**
Most special revenue funds are tied to a specific revenue source (e.g. the Tourist Development Fund to the Tourist Development Tax), but some funds receive transfers from other funds in addition to or instead of a dedicated revenue source.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"All County money is one pot the Board can spend on anything."** Most non-General-Fund revenue is legally restricted to the purpose of its own fund — see [Fund Descriptions](fund-descriptions.md) and [Revenue Guide](revenue-guide.md).
- **"A fund with a large balance has 'extra' money to spend freely."** A fund balance may be legally restricted, committed to a specific purpose, or held as a reserve required by policy — see [Reserves](reserves.md).
- **"Major and Non-Major means more or less important."** It is a financial reporting size threshold, not a statement about a service's importance.

# Fund Descriptions

## Purpose

This document describes the purpose, revenue sources, and typical uses of each of Walton County's 20 funds. See [Fund Structure](fund-structure.md) for why these funds exist and how they're categorized.

## Major Governmental Funds

### General Fund (001)
The County's primary operating fund. It accounts for financial resources not required to be reported in another fund, with major funding from ad valorem (property) taxes. The collection and disbursement of monies to the Constitutional Officers is also accounted for in this fund.

### Transportation Fund (101)
Accounts for motor fuel taxes, local option gas taxes, state revenue sharing, and grants supporting roadway construction, maintenance, drainage, and infrastructure operations.

### Sheriff Fund (107)
Accounts for fines, forfeitures, grants, and transfers supporting Sheriff's Office operations in accordance with Florida Statutes.

### Tourist Development Fund (111)
Supports tourism promotion, beach renourishment, visitor-serving infrastructure, and public safety initiatives, funded primarily by the Tourist Development Tax. See [Tourist Development Tax](tourist-development-tax.md).

### Solid Waste Fund (112)
Accounts for landfill operations, funded through County surtaxes, state grants, landfill fees, and scrap sales revenue.

### Capital Projects Fund (300)
Accounts for resources designated for acquiring, constructing, and improving major capital facilities and infrastructure. See [Capital Projects](capital-projects.md).

## Non-Major Governmental Funds

- **Debt Service Fund (201)**: accounts for the accumulation of resources for, and payment of, long-term debt principal and interest.
- **MSBU Fund (102)**: special assessments for infrastructure improvements within a Municipal Service Benefit Unit — a defined area where property owners are assessed for a specific local improvement.
- **Building Fund (103)**: fees and assessments supporting building compliance and permitting activities.
- **State Housing Initiative Program (SHIP) Fund (104)**: State Housing Initiatives Partnership Program grant resources supporting affordable housing programs.
- **Mosquito Control Fund (105)**: dependent district activities for mosquito and insect control.
- **Mosquito Control State Aid Fund (106)**: state grant resources supporting mosquito and insect control.
- **Law Enforcement Trust Fund (108)**: resources restricted to law enforcement purposes under Florida Statutes governing forfeiture proceeds.
- **E911 Fund (109)**: phone charges assessed for emergency assistance (911) communications services.
- **Housing & Urban Development Fund (110)**: federal Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) grant resources.
- **Preservation Fund (113)**: fees paid in lieu of reserved land, used for restoration of native vegetation on public lands.
- **Recreation Plat Fee Fund (114)**: developer assessments supporting recreational facilities.
- **Sidewalk Fund (115)**: assessments paid in lieu of constructing required sidewalks.
- **Mobility Fee Fund (116)**: fees supporting multi-modal transportation infrastructure tied to new development.

## Internal Service Fund

### Self-Insurance Fund (503)
An Internal Service Fund used to account for the financing of goods or services provided by one County department to other departments on a cost-reimbursement basis — in this case, self-insurance activities. This is a Proprietary Fund, not a Governmental Fund, because it operates on a cost-recovery, exchange-transaction basis rather than through tax revenue.

## Fund Types At A Glance

- **Governmental Funds**: core public services — general operations, transportation, public safety, tourism, capital projects, grants, assessments, and other restricted activities.
- **Proprietary Funds**: business-type or internal service activities, where costs are recovered through user charges or internal allocations.
- **Fiduciary Funds**: resources held by the County on behalf of other individuals, agencies, governments, or funds.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Which fund receives most of the County's property tax revenue?**
The General Fund — "the majority of funding is from ad valorem taxes."

**Can the Tourist Development Fund pay for a Sheriff's Office patrol car?**
Generally no, unless the specific use ties directly to the statutorily authorized purposes of the Tourist Development Tax (tourism promotion, beach/shoreline maintenance, and related visitor-impact public safety needs). Routine county-wide law enforcement is primarily funded through the Sheriff Fund and General Fund. See [Tourist Development Tax](tourist-development-tax.md) and [Common Misconceptions](common-misconceptions.md).

**What is an MSBU, and why would my property be assessed one?**
A Municipal Service Benefit Unit is a defined geographic area where property owners are assessed for a specific local improvement or service (such as road paving or drainage) that specifically benefits that area, rather than being funded countywide.

**Why does the E911 Fund exist separately from the Sheriff Fund, if the Sheriff's Office operates the E911 Communications Center?**
The E911 Fund tracks the specific 911-service phone charge revenue that legally must fund emergency communications infrastructure and operations, even though the Sheriff's Office is the department that operates the center day to day. Fund separation keeps that dedicated revenue's use transparent regardless of which department carries out the service. See [Department to Fund Relationship (live page)](/pages/department-to-fund-relationship.html).

## Common Misconceptions

- **"MSBU funds are secret extra taxes."** MSBU assessments fund specific, locally voted or petitioned improvements benefiting the assessed area directly, and are a standard Florida local government financing tool, not an additional general tax.
- **"The Self-Insurance Fund is 'profit' the County keeps."** It exists to pool and manage risk/claims costs across departments on a cost-recovery basis, not to generate revenue.

# Revenue Guide

## Purpose

This document explains each major revenue category Walton County collects: what it is, who pays it, why it exists, and what it can legally fund.

## Overview

Walton County's revenue is organized into seven categories on the Summary of Revenues (see [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md)):

1. General Government Taxes
2. Permits, Fees, and Special Assessments
3. Intergovernmental Revenues
4. Charges for Services
5. Judgments, Fines and Forfeits
6. Miscellaneous Revenue
7. Other Sources

A critical fact for understanding the budget: **most County revenue is not interchangeable.** Many sources are legally restricted to specific purposes, and even revenue collected in the same broad category can be tied to different funds with different legal restrictions. See [Fund Structure](fund-structure.md).

## General Government Taxes

"Ad valorem, tourist development, and other locally levied taxes."

- **Ad Valorem Taxes (property taxes)**: a tax levied on the assessed value (net of exemptions) of real or personal property. The rate is expressed as a **millage rate** — one mill equals $1 of tax per $1,000 of taxable value. Ad valorem tax is the County's largest General Fund revenue source and is largely unrestricted, supporting general government operations, public safety, constitutional officers, and other countywide services. See [Property Tax](property-tax.md).
- **Tourist Development Tax**: established by Florida Statute 125.0104 in 1977, giving counties the option to raise additional revenue for tourism-related projects or activities. It is paid by visitors renting short-term lodging, not by county residents' property, and is legally restricted to tourism-related uses. See [Tourist Development Tax](tourist-development-tax.md).
- **Local Discretionary Sales Surtax**: a county-levied discretionary sales surtax authorized under Florida law.
- **Local Option Fuel Tax**: a locally levied tax on motor fuel, restricted to transportation purposes. See [Fuel Taxes](fuel-taxes.md).

## Permits, Fees, and Special Assessments

Revenue from permits, licenses, and special assessments — including building permits, beach activity permits, and Municipal Service Benefit Unit (MSBU) assessments. **Special Assessments** are compulsory levies against certain properties to defray the cost of specific improvements or services presumed to be of general benefit to the public and of special benefit to the assessed property (e.g. a paving or drainage project in a specific neighborhood).

## Intergovernmental Revenues

"Funds received from federal, state and other local government sources in the form of grants, shared revenues, and payments in lieu of taxes."

- **State Shared Revenues**: revenues collected by the State of Florida and proportionately shared with counties on the basis of specific formulas, including the Local Government Half-Cent Sales Tax, Mobile Home Licenses, Alcoholic Beverage Licenses, and Insurance Agents County Licenses.
- **Sales Tax (state-collected)**: taxes imposed on the sale of designated goods, rentals, and services under Section 212.054, Florida Statutes. These are collected and retained by the State, except for the Local Government Half-Cent Sales Tax Program, which returns funds to counties and cities by formula for any lawful purpose.
- **Federal and State Grants**: awarded for specific programs; grant revenue is generally added to the budget after the grant agreement is executed. See [Grants](grants.md).

## Charges for Services

Fees charged for specific County services rendered directly to residents and businesses — for example, ambulance fees, planning fees, recreation program fees, and golf course revenue at County-operated facilities.

## Judgments, Fines and Forfeits

Revenue from court judgments, fines, and forfeitures, including ordinance fines.

## Miscellaneous Revenue

Interest earnings, donations, and other revenue not classified elsewhere — including interest income on invested County funds, indirect administrative fee reimbursements between funds, and proceeds from the sale of surplus or fixed assets.

## Other Sources

Transfers in, debt proceeds, and other non-recurring funding sources — for example, a fund's beginning balance carried forward, or a transfer from another fund. See [Interfund Transfers](/pages/summary-of-interfund-transfers.html).

## Revenue Restrictions in Practice

| Revenue Source | Typical Restriction |
|---|---|
| Ad Valorem Taxes | Largely unrestricted (General Fund); some levies dedicated to specific funds (e.g. Sheriff Fund) |
| Tourist Development Tax | Tourism promotion, beach/shoreline maintenance, tourism-related public safety |
| Fuel Taxes | Transportation (roads, drainage, related infrastructure) |
| Impact Fees | The specific type of infrastructure the fee was charged for (e.g. transportation impact fees for roads) |
| MSBU/Special Assessments | The specific local improvement the assessment funds |
| E911 Fees | Emergency communications infrastructure and operations |
| Grants | The specific program or project named in the grant agreement |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Where does Walton County get most of its money?**
Ad valorem (property) taxes are the largest source of General Fund revenue; the Tourist Development Tax is a major source of tourism-restricted revenue. See the [Summary of Revenues (live page)](/pages/summary-of-revenues.html) for current-year totals by category.

**How much comes from tourism?**
Tourist Development Tax collections fund tourism promotion, beach management, and related uses through the Tourist Development Fund — see [Tourist Development Tax](tourist-development-tax.md) for the current total.

**Can tourist taxes pay for general government operations, like the courthouse or general administration?**
No. The Tourist Development Tax is restricted by Florida Statute 125.0104 to tourism-related purposes. See [Common Misconceptions](common-misconceptions.md).

**Why are grants counted as revenue instead of just a pass-through?**
A grant is real revenue received by the County, spent on the specific purpose the grant funds — it is budgeted as both a revenue (the grant award) and a corresponding expenditure (the funded program), which is standard governmental accounting practice.

**Why do some funds show revenue every year while others fluctuate wildly?**
Recurring revenue sources like property taxes are stable year to year; sources like grants, asset sale proceeds, or debt proceeds are one-time and can vary significantly by year depending on what was awarded or occurred that year.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Every dollar the County collects can be spent on anything."** Most revenue outside the General Fund's ad valorem tax base is legally restricted to a specific purpose. See [Fund Structure](fund-structure.md).
- **"Impact fees can be spent anywhere the County needs money."** Impact fees must fund the specific type of infrastructure they were charged for, tied to the impact of new development.
- **"Grants are free money with no obligations."** Grants come with specific program requirements, reporting obligations, and often a required local funding match — see [Grants](grants.md).

# Expenditure Guide

## Purpose

This document explains each major expenditure category Walton County budgets: what it is, why it exists, and examples of what it funds.

## Overview

Walton County's expenditures are organized into six categories, shown consistently across department, fund, and countywide summary tables (see [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md)):

1. Personnel Services
2. Operating Expenditures
3. Capital Outlay
4. Debt Service
5. Grants and Aid
6. Other Uses

Expenditures are also grouped by **activity** — a broader functional classification spanning departments, used to answer "how much does the County spend on Public Safety?" regardless of which department or fund the spending sits in. Walton County's expenditure activities are: General Government, Public Safety, Physical Environment, Transportation, Economic Environment, Human Services, Culture and Recreation, Court-Related Cost, and Other Uses.

## Personnel Services

"Covers employee compensation and benefits, including salaries, overtime, weekend and holiday pay, seasonal workers, FICA, Florida Retirement System (FRS) contributions, health insurance, workers' compensation, life insurance, and paid leave buybacks."

Personnel Services is typically the largest expenditure category for public-safety- and service-delivery-heavy departments, since County services (law enforcement, libraries, parks, permitting) are fundamentally delivered by people. See [Personnel](personnel.md) and [Salary Guide](salary-guide.md).

## Operating Expenditures

"Covers the day-to-day costs of providing County services, including utilities, fuel, maintenance, professional services, software, office supplies, communications, training, and other routine operating expenses."

This is the category for the recurring, non-personnel cost of keeping a service running day to day.

## Capital Outlay

"Covers major investments in long-term County assets, including vehicles, machinery and equipment, technology systems, buildings, facility improvements, roads, drainage, parks, and other infrastructure projects."

Capital Outlay covers individual fixed-asset purchases; larger, multi-year capital construction projects are tracked in more detail through the [Capital Improvement Plan](capital-projects.md).

## Debt Service

"Covers principal and interest payments on County debt obligations, including bonds, loans, and other long-term financing arrangements."

Debt Service is how the County repays money borrowed (typically through bonds) to fund large capital projects over time rather than paying the full cost in a single year. See [Debt](debt.md).

## Grants and Aid

"Covers funding provided to other governments, agencies, and organizations through grants, aid payments, and other transfers in support of County program objectives." This includes the Non-Profit Funding Program and statutory aid to other agencies.

## Other Uses

"Covers transfers, reserves, and other budgetary uses not classified as personnel, operating, capital, debt service, or grants and aid expenditures." This includes Interfund Transfers and contingency reserves. See [Budget Terminology](budget-terminology.md).

## Expenditure Activities (Functional Groupings)

- **General Government**: Board of County Commissioners, County Administration, Office of Management and Budget, Office of the County Attorney, Human Resources, Procurement, Geographic Info Systems, and similar administrative/constitutional-officer functions supporting the whole organization.
- **Public Safety**: Sheriff's Office (including corrections, law enforcement, animal services, and fire rescue — see [Constitutional Officers](constitutional-officers.md)), Emergency Management, Code Compliance, Building Department, Medical Examiner, and related functions. See [Public Safety](public-safety.md).
- **Physical Environment**: Solid Waste, Environmental Resources, Extension Office, Soil Conservation, and MSBU-funded infrastructure.
- **Transportation**: Public Works, Engineering, Capital Projects tied to roads, and the Sidewalk Fund. See [Transportation](transportation.md).
- **Economic Environment**: Tourism-related departments (Tourism Administration, Beach Operations, Marketing, Beach Renourishment, Beach Tram, Sales and Visitors Center, Communications), Housing & Urban Development, and Veteran Services. See [Tourism](tourism.md) and [Economic Development](economic-development.md).
- **Human Services**: Mosquito Control, Non-Profit Funding Program, and Walton County Health Department support.
- **Culture and Recreation**: Libraries, Recreation, Eagle Springs Golf and Recreation Center, and the Recreation Plat Fee Fund. See [Parks and Recreation](parks-and-recreation.md) and [Libraries](libraries.md).
- **Court-Related Cost**: Circuit Court, County Court, Court Technology, Court Innovations, Guardian Ad Litem, Public Defender, and State Attorney support.
- **Other Uses**: transfers and contingency items not tied to a direct service delivery activity.

## Recurring vs. One-Time Expenditures

- **Recurring expenditures** are ongoing costs expected every year (most Personnel Services and Operating Expenditures).
- **One-time expenditures** are costs that do not repeat (most Capital Outlay and many Grants and Aid items).

A sound budget practice — reflected in Walton County's [Financial Policies](financial-policies.md) — is funding recurring expenditures with recurring revenue, and using one-time revenue (like a grant or asset sale) primarily for one-time expenditures, since one-time revenue cannot be relied on to repeat and fund the same ongoing cost next year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What does the County spend the most on?**
This varies by fund and activity; see the [Summary of Expenses (live page)](/pages/summary-of-expenses.html) for current totals by category and activity, and individual [department pages](departments.md) for department-specific figures.

**Why is a road project budgeted as Capital Outlay in one place and shown under Transportation activity in another?**
Capital Outlay is the expenditure *type* (what kind of cost it is); Transportation is the expenditure *activity* (what functional purpose it serves). The same dollar is correctly described both ways — see [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md).

**How are salaries determined?**
Personnel Services costs reflect County pay structures, negotiated or Board-approved compensation plans, and state-mandated contributions (like FRS retirement contributions and FICA) — see [Personnel](personnel.md) and [Salary Guide](salary-guide.md).

**What happens to unspent operating funds at the end of the year?**
Unspent, unencumbered operating appropriations generally lapse at fiscal year-end rather than automatically carrying forward, though specific project or grant balances may be carried forward — see [Budget Terminology](budget-terminology.md) (Carry Forward) and [Budget Process](budget-process.md).

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Capital Outlay is wasteful spending."** It funds long-term assets (vehicles, buildings, infrastructure) the County needs to keep delivering services — deferring it typically costs more later through deterioration or emergency replacement.
- **"Personnel Services costs are just salaries."** They also include mandatory benefits like FICA, Florida Retirement System contributions, health insurance, and workers' compensation — costs largely outside the County's discretion to change unilaterally.
- **"Grants and Aid money isn't really the County's spending."** It is a real, appropriated expenditure that supports County program objectives, even though it flows to another organization.

# Departments Overview

## Purpose

This document lists Walton County's departments and autonomous entities, organized by service area, with a factual summary of what each one does drawn directly from the County's own published department narratives. For the five separately elected offices, see [Constitutional Officers](constitutional-officers.md) instead.

## Overview

Walton County's budget includes three groups of organizations:

1. **Board-governed departments** — report to the County Administrator, who reports to the Board of County Commissioners.
2. **Constitutional Officers** — five independently elected offices. See [Constitutional Officers](constitutional-officers.md).
3. **Autonomous entities and state-mandated cost-sharing** — courts, court-related programs, the Health Department, and other functions the County is statutorily required to help fund but does not directly operate.

## General Government

- **County Administration**: executes the directives and priorities set by the Board of County Commissioners, serving as the central hub of communication and coordination and the primary interface for County citizens.
- **Office of Management and Budget (OMB)**: provides financial and administrative support to the Board, oversees all authorized funds to ensure proper utilization, and advises department and division heads.
- **Office of the County Attorney**: provides legal services to the County across three primary areas — contracts, litigation, and public records.
- **Human Resources**: provides centralized personnel services for all County departments, including recruitment, performance management, discipline policy standardization, employee development, and workers' compensation administration.
- **Purchasing**: manages purchasing activities in compliance with federal, state, and local law, aiming to secure goods and services at favorable prices while maintaining quality and service standards.
- **Geographic Info Systems (GIS)**: manages the County's Geographic Information System and provides geographic data, products, and services to county offices, other agencies, and the public.

## Public Safety

- **Sheriff's Office**: law enforcement, corrections, court services, animal control, fire rescue, and 911 dispatch — see [Constitutional Officers](constitutional-officers.md) for full detail.

- **Engineering Department**: manages the design and construction of County infrastructure projects, including capital improvement design/construction management, traffic operations, right-of-way permitting, surveying, and infrastructure grant administration.

- **Tourism Administration**: administers Tourist Development Tax-funded projects, focused on maintaining a stable, strong tourism revenue base and managing visitation consistently across the year. See [Tourism](tourism.md).
- **Housing & Urban Development**: administers the federally funded Section 8 tenant-based Housing Choice Voucher assistance program for Walton County as a public housing agency.
- **Veteran Services**: administers and advocates for benefits earned by veterans and their dependents in Walton County, providing customer service reflecting gratitude for their service.

## Court-Related and Statutory Obligations

Florida law requires counties to help fund certain judicial and state-mandated functions, even though the County does not operate them directly:

- **Circuit Court / County Court**: judges and judicial assistants are state employees, but the County is responsible for reasonable salaries of court secretaries/assistants and reasonable judicial expenses when the State does not cover them.
- **Court Technology**: state law (Section 29.008(1)(f)2 and (h)) allocates a portion of certain fees to the county for court technology costs.
- **Court Innovations**: funded by a $65 court cost imposed on felony, misdemeanor, and criminal traffic convictions under Walton County Ordinance 2004-22 and Florida Statute § 939.185, supplementing state funding and county obligations.
- **Guardian Ad Litem**: Article V of the Florida Constitution and Chapter 29.008, Florida Statutes require counties to fund staffing, communications, and facility costs for the Guardian ad Litem program, which appoints volunteers to protect children's rights and interests in court proceedings.
- **Public Defender**: represents indigent defendants under Section 27.52, Florida Statutes; the County must provide office space, utilities, and related support services under Section 27.54.
- **State Attorney**: elected per judicial circuit; prosecutes/defends on behalf of the State in circuit and county courts within the circuit.
- **Statutory & Other Agency Funding**: Board-allocated support for statutory and agency services including mental health services, volunteer fire departments, healthcare response initiatives, and redevelopment efforts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why does the County fund the Circuit and County Courts if judges are state employees?**
Florida law makes the County responsible for reasonable salaries of court secretaries/assistants and reasonable judicial expenses whenever the State does not directly cover them — a cost-sharing arrangement between state and county government.

**Is the Walton County Health Department a County department?**
It operates as a public health unit under a contractual agreement between the County and the State of Florida, per Chapter 154, Florida Statutes — a hybrid arrangement rather than a standard Board department.

**Why does the County fund the Public Defender's office space if the Public Defender is a state position?**
Section 27.54, Florida Statutes requires counties to provide office space, utilities, and related support services to the Public Defender, even though the position itself is a state judicial-circuit office.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Every line item in the County budget is a County-run department."** Several budget lines (Circuit Court, State Attorney, Public Defender, Guardian ad Litem, Medical Examiner) fund state-mandated cost-sharing obligations for offices the County does not operate.
- **"South Walton Fire is a County department."** It is a separate fire district; the County's budget line accounts for the cost of an interlocal service agreement with that district.

# Constitutional Officers

## Purpose

This document explains what a Constitutional Officer is, why Walton County's five Constitutional Officers are treated differently from Board-governed departments, and what each office does.

## Overview

Florida's Constitution establishes five county offices that are filled by separate, direct election rather than appointment: the **Clerk of Courts & County Comptroller, Property Appraiser, Sheriff, Supervisor of Elections, and Tax Collector**. Each is a **Constitutional Officer** — legally independent of the Board of County Commissioners (BCC) and the County Administrator, even though each office's budget is included in the County's overall annual budget for appropriation purposes.

Once the Board approves a Constitutional Officer's budget, that office "operates as a separate entity, setting its own policies and procedures, employee hiring and line-item budget management" — a phrase that appears, in substance, in each office's own official narrative. The Board does not direct how a Constitutional Officer spends their appropriated budget line by line, hires staff, or sets internal office procedures.

## The Five Constitutional Officers

### Clerk of Courts & County Comptroller
A separately elected Constitutional Officer who also acts as Clerk to the Board of County Commissioners. Supports judicial functions through the Civil Division, Family Services, Felony Division, Jury Administration, Juvenile and Recording Divisions. As Clerk to the Board, also provides Finance, Information Systems, and BCC recording functions — including accounting and auditing services, contract and purchase disbursements, investment of Board funds, information systems support for court and accounting services, and maintaining the County's "Official Records." The Clerk's budget is included in the County's General Fund for the portion of operations not covered by service fees.

### Property Appraiser
A separately elected Constitutional Officer whose budget is approved by the Florida Department of Revenue. Florida law requires the Board of County Commissioners to pay municipalities' and the school board's share of the Property Appraiser's budget cost. Responsible for determining the value of all property within the County, maintaining related records, calculating tax on taxable property once taxes are levied, and distributing Truth-in-Millage (TRIM) notices.

### Sheriff's Office
A separately elected Constitutional Officer whose functions include Corrections, Court Services, Law Enforcement, Animal Services, and Fire Rescue. The Corrections division operates the County detention facility (inmate housing, medical services, food services, prisoner transportation). Court Services provides bailiffs, courtroom security, and jury/judicial protective services. Law Enforcement includes uniformed road patrol, criminal investigations, crime scene investigation, K-9 units, and the E911 Communications Center. **The Walton County Sheriff's Office has also taken on the operation of the animal control facility and units, and the operations of Walton County Fire Rescue Services within the county** — meaning animal control, fire rescue, and 911 dispatch are all Sheriff's Office functions in Walton County, not separate agencies. See [Public Safety](public-safety.md).

### Supervisor of Elections
A separately elected Constitutional Officer whose overall budget is included in the General Fund. Administers elections and maintains voter registration records and statistics, ensuring compliance with the federally mandated National Voter Registration Act — including coordinating and training poll workers, setting up precinct polling places, ballot printing, and providing voting booths meeting minimum standards under Florida Statutes.

### Tax Collector
A separately elected Constitutional Officer whose budget is approved by the Florida Department of Revenue. Fee charges to the County's various taxing authorities are based on a legislated percentage of taxes collected for each authority. The Office's primary role is as an agent of state government: administering vehicle and vessel titling/registration as an agent of the Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles, issuing hunting and fishing licenses as an agent for the Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission, collecting sales tax on vehicle and vessel sales as an agent for the Florida Department of Revenue, and collecting, investing, and distributing real and tangible property taxes (including delinquent tax collection) and occupational license tax as an agent of local and state government.

## Why This Matters for the Budget

- **Budget appropriation vs. budget control**: the Board decides how much total funding each Constitutional Officer's office receives, but does not control how that office spends it internally.
- **Different approval authorities**: the Property Appraiser's and Tax Collector's budgets are approved by the Florida Department of Revenue, not solely the Board — a state-level check unique to those offices.
- **Distinct legal responsibilities**: each office performs functions specifically assigned to it by Florida Statutes or the Florida Constitution, separate from the general governmental functions the Board and County Administrator oversee.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the County Administrator supervise the Sheriff?**
No. The Sheriff is an independently elected Constitutional Officer, not an employee of the County Administrator or Board.

**Why are animal control, fire rescue, and 911 dispatch part of the Sheriff's Office budget rather than separate departments?**
In Walton County, the Sheriff's Office has taken on operational responsibility for the animal control facility/units, Walton County Fire Rescue Services, and the E911 Communications Center, in addition to its traditional law enforcement, corrections, and court services functions.

**Who approves the Property Appraiser's and Tax Collector's budgets?**
The Florida Department of Revenue approves both, reflecting the state-level regulatory role those two offices play in the property tax system.

**Can the Board cut a Constitutional Officer's budget?**
The Board approves the total appropriation for each Constitutional Officer during the annual budget process, but Florida law provides specific procedures and protections for how those budgets are set and disputed, differing from how a Board-governed department's budget is set.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Constitutional Officers are County departments."** They are independently elected offices operating under their own authority once budgeted — see [Mission and Purpose](mission.md).
- **"The Sheriff's Office only does law enforcement."** In Walton County it also runs corrections, court security, animal control, fire rescue, and 911 dispatch.
- **"The Board can tell the Sheriff how to spend the Sheriff Fund."** The Board appropriates the total; the Sheriff's Office manages its own line-item spending, hiring, and internal policy within that appropriation.

## Definitions

- **Constitutional Officer**: an office established directly by the Florida Constitution and filled by separate election, operating independently of the Board of County Commissioners once its budget is set.

# Financial Policies

## Purpose

This document summarizes Walton County's adopted financial policies — the rules that govern how the County manages reserves, prepares budgets, handles cash, administers grants, and safeguards public assets. Each summary here reflects the County's own published policy summary; the full adopted policy documents are linked from each section.

## Overview

The Board of County Commissioners has adopted financial policies to promote fiscal stability, transparency, accountability, and compliance with Florida law. These policies govern how the budget is built, monitored, and amended, and how the County protects public funds and assets.

## Fund Balance Policy

Establishes guidelines for maintaining adequate fund balances to support financial stability, liquidity, emergency preparedness, investment-grade credit ratings, and long-term fiscal sustainability. The policy defines the County's fund balance classifications and establishes reserve targets designed to protect the County's financial position. See [Reserves](reserves.md).

## Budget Policy

Provides the framework for preparing, adopting, and administering a balanced budget in accordance with Florida law. The policy promotes long-term planning, fiscal accountability, and responsible allocation of County resources. See [Budget Process](budget-process.md).

## Cash Handling Policy

Establishes internal controls and accountability measures for the receipt, safeguarding, deposit, and management of County funds to protect public assets and reduce financial risk.

## Grants Administration

Provides guidance for the administration, monitoring, and reporting of grant-funded activities to ensure compliance with applicable grant requirements and maximize the effective use of external funding sources. See [Grants](grants.md).

## Budget Transfers and Amendments

Establishes procedures for budget transfers and amendments in accordance with Florida Statutes, ensuring transparency, fiscal control, and appropriate authorization of budget changes. See [Budget Process](budget-process.md) for the statutory amendment process under Section 129.06, Florida Statutes.

## Capital Asset Policy

Provides standards for the acquisition, capitalization, inventory, maintenance, and disposal of County assets to ensure accountability and effective management of public resources. See [Expenditure Guide](expenditure-guide.md) (Capital Outlay).

## Anti-Fraud and Whistleblower Policies

Promotes ethical conduct, transparency, and accountability by establishing procedures for reporting suspected fraud, waste, abuse, or misconduct, and protecting individuals who report concerns in good faith.

## Investment Policy

Establishes guidelines for investing County funds with emphasis on safety, liquidity, diversification, and yield, while preserving principal and complying with Florida law.

## Indirect Administrative Cost Allocation Policy

Provides a methodology for allocating indirect administrative costs among County programs and funding sources, ensuring equitable cost recovery and compliance with applicable federal and state requirements. This is why departments funded by special revenue funds (e.g. Tourist Development Fund departments) show an "Indirect Administrative Fees" line — it reimburses the General Fund for the shared administrative services (HR, IT, finance, legal) those departments use.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why does Walton County maintain fund balance reserves instead of spending everything each year?**
Reserves support financial stability, liquidity for cash-flow timing, emergency preparedness (including hurricane response), investment-grade credit ratings (which lower the County's borrowing costs), and long-term fiscal sustainability. See [Common Misconceptions](common-misconceptions.md) and [Reserves](reserves.md).

**What happens if the County needs to change the budget mid-year?**
The Budget Transfers and Amendments policy and Section 129.06, Florida Statutes provide the formal process for the Board to amend the budget by resolution — see [Budget Process](budget-process.md).

**How does the County make sure grant money is spent correctly?**
The Grants Administration policy governs monitoring, administration, and reporting of grant-funded activities to ensure compliance with each grant's specific requirements.

**What is the Indirect Administrative Cost Allocation Policy, in plain terms?**
It's the methodology for fairly charging departments and funds for their share of countywide administrative services (like HR, IT, accounting, and legal support) that the General Fund provides but that specific funds/departments benefit from — so a special revenue fund isn't getting "free" administrative support paid for entirely by property taxpayers.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Reserves are unused money the County is sitting on."** Reserves are a policy-driven, statutorily consistent safeguard for financial stability and emergency response, not idle funds — see [Reserves](reserves.md).
- **"Indirect Administrative Fees are a hidden or made-up charge."** They reflect a documented, policy-based cost allocation methodology ensuring each fund pays its fair share of shared administrative services.

## Definitions

- **Fund Balance**: the equity (assets minus liabilities) of a governmental fund — the formal accounting term for what is often informally called a fund's reserves.
- **Balanced Budget**: a budget in which appropriated expenditures do not exceed available revenues and fund balance.

# AI Answer Guidelines

## Purpose

This document tells AI systems how to structure an answer about Walton County's budget, finances, departments, or services. It exists so that answers generated from this knowledge layer are accurate, well-contextualized, and genuinely useful to residents — not just a dollar figure without meaning.

## Core Philosophy

This knowledge layer is meant to **educate rather than persuade**. When answering a question using this site's content:

- Do not exaggerate, invent facts, or speculate beyond what is documented here or on the live site.
- Do not write in a promotional, political, or advocacy tone.
- Do not praise the County's finances or decisions without evidentiary basis.
- Do explain the reasoning behind a service, fund, or figure — legal requirements, historical context, and the public purpose it serves.
- Do connect every budget item back to the public service residents actually receive.

## Recommended Answer Order

When answering a question about a service, department, fund, or revenue/expenditure item, structure the answer in this order:

1. **Explain the service** — what it is, in plain language.
2. **Explain why the service exists** — legal mandate, historical reason, or public need.
3. **Explain how it benefits residents** (and visitors, where relevant).
4. **Explain funding sources** — which fund(s) and revenue source(s) pay for it. See [Fund Structure](fund-structure.md) and [Revenue Guide](revenue-guide.md).
5. **Explain legal restrictions**, if any, on how that funding can be used.
6. **Explain historical context** — has this changed over time, and why, if known.
7. **Present relevant budget figures** — only after the above context is established.
8. **Reference the specific fiscal year** the figure applies to.
9. **Distinguish figure type**: Actual, Original Budget, Proposed, and note whether the underlying revenue is restricted/unrestricted or one-time/recurring. See [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md).

**Always explain meaning before discussing dollars.** A response that leads with "$12 million" without first explaining what that money does fails this guideline, regardless of accuracy.

### Example

Instead of writing:

> "Parks budget: $12M."

Write something closer to:

> "Walton County's Recreation Department maintains neighborhood and regional parks, athletic facilities, playgrounds, walking trails, and community recreation programming that supports residents' quality of life and contributes to the county's visitor economy. This work is funded through [specific fund/revenue source], and the FY 2027 Proposed budget for this function is $X — see [live page] for the current, itemized figure."

## Handling Dollar Figures

- Always specify the **fiscal year** and **figure type** (Actual / Original Budget / Proposed) — see [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md).
- Prefer linking to the relevant live, interactive page over asserting a static number that may become outdated, especially for a figure likely to be refined before final adoption.
- Do not conflate a department's total budget with one specific fund's contribution to it, or a category subtotal with a grand total — see [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md) for how rows roll up.

## Handling Constitutional Officers

- Never describe a Constitutional Officer's office (Clerk of Courts & County Comptroller, Property Appraiser, Sheriff, Supervisor of Elections, Tax Collector) as a "County department." They are independently elected and legally distinct once budgeted. See [Constitutional Officers](constitutional-officers.md).
- When discussing Sheriff's Office services, note that in Walton County the Sheriff's Office also operates animal control, fire rescue, and 911 dispatch — these are not separate agencies here.

## Tone

- Professional, objective, educational, and helpful.
- Plain English first; government finance terminology introduced with a definition (see [Budget Terminology](budget-terminology.md)).
- No advocacy, no political framing, no emotional language, no marketing language.
- Write in the register of a Government Finance Officers Association (GFOA) budget book: precise, sourced, and calm.

## What Not to Do

- Do not fabricate a specific dollar figure, legal citation, or department claim not grounded in this site's documented content.
- Do not speculate about future political decisions, elections, or personnel.
- Do not offer opinions on whether a tax rate or spending level is "too high" or "too low."
- Do not present a proposed (not-yet-adopted) figure as final.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Only after explaining the service's purpose and funding context — and only when confident in the figure's currency; otherwise, link to the live source page.

**How should an AI system handle a question this knowledge layer doesn't cover?**

# Common Misconceptions About Walton County's Budget

## Purpose

This document directly addresses frequent misunderstandings about how Walton County's budget works, why money can't always move freely between purposes, and why figures that look inconsistent at first glance usually are not.

## Why Can't Tourist Development Tax Revenue Pay for Everything?

The Tourist Development Tax was established by Florida Statute 125.0104 in 1977 specifically to give counties an option to raise revenue for tourism-related projects or activities. Because the tax is authorized for that specific purpose, it is deposited into the Tourist Development Fund and can only be used for tourism promotion, beach/shoreline maintenance and renourishment, and related tourism-impact needs — not for unrelated general government functions like the courthouse, general administration, or countywide law enforcement patrol. See [Tourist Development Tax](tourist-development-tax.md) and [Fund Descriptions](fund-descriptions.md).

## Why Doesn't the County Spend All of Its Reserve Money?

The County's Fund Balance Policy establishes reserve targets to support financial stability, liquidity, emergency preparedness (including hurricane response), investment-grade credit ratings, and long-term fiscal sustainability. A credit rating downgrade from depleted reserves would raise the County's borrowing costs on future debt, ultimately costing taxpayers more. Reserves are a deliberate safeguard, not idle or forgotten money. See [Reserves](reserves.md) and [Financial Policies](financial-policies.md).

## Why Are Grants Listed as Revenue?

A grant awarded to the County is real revenue — money the County receives and is authorized to spend on the grant's specific purpose. It is budgeted as both a revenue (the grant award) and a matching expenditure (the funded program), which is standard governmental accounting practice, not double-counting. See [Grants](grants.md).

## Why Are Constitutional Officers Shown Separately?

The Clerk of Courts & County Comptroller, Property Appraiser, Sheriff, Supervisor of Elections, and Tax Collector are independently elected under the Florida Constitution. Their budgets appear within the County's overall budget because the Board of County Commissioners appropriates their funding, but each office sets its own internal policies, hiring, and line-item spending independently once its budget is approved — a legal distinction from Board-governed departments that the presentation is designed to make clear. See [Constitutional Officers](constitutional-officers.md).

## Why Do Actuals Differ From Budgets?

A budget is a financial plan prepared months before the fiscal year it covers; an Actual is the closed, historical record of what really happened. Staffing vacancies, project timing, emergency response, weather events, and revenue performance all cause real variance between a plan and reality. Variance between Actual and Budget is normal and does not by itself indicate a problem. See [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md).

## Why Is a Project Budgeted Over Multiple Years?

Capital projects — road construction, facility improvements, infrastructure upgrades — often take longer than one fiscal year to design, permit, and build. The Capital Improvement Plan budgets these projects across the years they are expected to be active, rather than forcing a multi-year project's full cost into a single year's budget. See [Capital Projects](capital-projects.md).

## Why Do Revenues Increase When Expenditures Increase (or Vice Versa)?

A budget must balance: appropriated expenditures cannot exceed available revenue and fund balance under the County's Budget Policy. When a department's expenditures grow (for example, due to new state mandates, inflation in operating costs, or an approved service expansion), the funding to support that growth has to come from somewhere — increased revenue, reallocated funding, or fund balance. The two sides of the budget move together because the budget is required to balance.

## Why Can't Impact Fees Be Spent Anywhere?

Impact fees are charged to developers at the time of development specifically to fund the construction of infrastructure needed to serve that development (roads, parks, etc.). Florida law requires a demonstrated connection ("rational nexus") between the fee charged and the infrastructure it funds, so impact fee revenue is restricted to the category of infrastructure it was collected for. See [Impact Fees](impact-fees.md).

## Why Do Some Funds Have Surpluses?

A fund can show a surplus (revenue exceeding expenditures in a given year) for reasons including: a project's spending is delayed into the next fiscal year, a one-time revenue source (like a grant or asset sale) arrived without an immediately corresponding expenditure, or the fund is intentionally building toward a reserve target under the Fund Balance Policy. A surplus in one year does not necessarily mean that fund is over-collecting on an ongoing basis.

## Why Isn't Every Dollar Interchangeable?

Because Walton County uses fund accounting specifically to keep legally restricted revenue separate from unrestricted revenue. A dollar of gas tax revenue is legally different from a dollar of ad valorem tax revenue, even though both are "County money" — one can only fund transportation, the other can fund general government purposes. Treating all County revenue as one interchangeable pool would misrepresent how public funds are legally required to be managed. See [Fund Structure](fund-structure.md).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If the County has money in reserves, why would it ever raise taxes or fees?**
Reserves are sized to specific policy targets for emergencies and financial stability, not to serve as a substitute for adequate ongoing (recurring) revenue to fund ongoing (recurring) services. Using reserves to avoid a needed, recurring revenue adjustment would deplete the reserve without solving the underlying funding gap.

**Does a "proposed" budget number mean the same thing as a final number?**
No — see [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md) for the distinction between Proposed and Adopted budgets.

**If my property's assessed value went up, does that mean the County is collecting more tax on purpose?**
Property value changes are determined by the Property Appraiser under state law, independent of the Board's millage rate decision. See [Property Tax](property-tax.md) and the site's glossary FAQ on this exact topic.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Purpose

It combines the site's official glossary/FAQ page content with additional grounded answers drawn from this knowledge layer. ## About the Budget

**What is the purpose of the County budget?**
The budget is an annual financial plan for Walton County. It specifies the level of services to be provided in the coming year as set by the Board of County Commissioners.

**How and when is the budget prepared?**
Florida law requires an annual budget, including all funds required by law, to be prepared, approved, and adopted for each fiscal year, in accordance with Chapters 125, 129, 200, and 218 of the Florida Statutes. For Walton County, the process begins in February and ends in September with legal adoption of the budget. See [Budget Process](budget-process.md).

**What is an operating budget?**

# Frequently Discussed Budget Topics

## Purpose

This document objectively explains topics that often come up in public discussion about Walton County's budget and finances. It presents factual background and process information only — it does not advocate for or against any position, criticize or defend any decision, or speculate about outcomes. Where a topic involves genuine public disagreement, this document explains the process by which the disagreement is resolved (public hearings, Board votes, statutory procedures) rather than taking a side.

## Why Do Some Residents Believe Taxes Are Too High?

Views on tax levels are a matter of public and political opinion, which this site does not take a position on. What can be factually explained is how the tax is calculated and adopted: ad valorem (property) tax equals the millage rate (set annually by the Board of County Commissioners) multiplied by taxable value (determined independently by the Property Appraiser). Florida's Truth in Millage (TRIM) law requires public notice and hearings before the Board adopts a final millage rate each year, and the "rolled-back rate" — the rate that would generate the same revenue as the prior year given new property values — is the standard reference point used statewide to describe whether a proposed rate constitutes a real increase. See [Why Are My Property Taxes Increasing?](intents/why-property-taxes-increase.md).

## How Are Millage Rates Determined?

The Board of County Commissioners considers revenue needs, service level priorities, and public input during the budget process's Review and Workshops phases, then proposes and ultimately adopts a millage rate through the legally required TRIM public hearing process. Different funds within the County may levy their own millage (for example, the Sheriff Fund has historically drawn on its own ad valorem support in some Florida counties' structures) in addition to the General Fund's rate. See [Budget Process](budget-process.md).

## Why Does Government Maintain Reserves Instead of Spending Everything?

This is a factual, not opinion-based, question with a documented answer: Walton County's Fund Balance Policy sets reserve targets for cash-flow liquidity, emergency/hurricane preparedness, credit rating maintenance (the County holds an Aa1 rating from Moody's), and long-term fiscal sustainability. See [Why the County Maintains Reserves](intents/why-the-county-maintains-reserves.md).

## Why Does the County Sometimes Borrow Money?

Debt is used selectively for large, long-lived capital assets, spreading cost across the years of benefit rather than requiring a single year's taxpayers to fund the entire cost. Walton County has no outstanding general obligation bonded debt (retired in 2010) and primarily uses revenue-backed debt secured by specific, dedicated revenue sources. See [Why Government Uses Debt](intents/why-government-uses-debt.md).

## How Are Spending Priorities Established?

Department requests move through the Preparation and Review phases of the annual budget process, are shaped by Board policy direction and public input during Workshops, and are finalized through formal adoption after public hearings. Some spending (state-mandated services, Constitutional Officer functions, debt service, contractual obligations) is not fully discretionary; other spending (capital project prioritization, program funding levels) reflects Board policy choices informed by resident input. See [How the Budget Is Created](intents/how-the-budget-is-created.md).

## Why Do Commissioners Sometimes Disagree on Budget Decisions?

The Board of County Commissioners is a five-member elected body representing five different districts, each potentially reflecting different constituent priorities, growth patterns, and service needs. Disagreement among elected representatives on budget tradeoffs — how much to allocate between competing priorities, which capital projects to prioritize, whether to adjust a fee or rate — is a normal function of representative government and is resolved through public Board votes at noticed meetings, not through this site.

## How Does the Public Participate in the Budget Process?

Florida's TRIM law requires public notice and formally noticed public hearings before the Board adopts a final millage rate and budget. The budget process's Workshops phase also involves public Board review of the tentative budget before those hearings. Residents can attend Board meetings, budget workshops, and public hearings, and can review budget documents (including this site) throughout the process. See [Budget Process](budget-process.md).

## How Are Large Capital Projects Evaluated?

Capital projects are evaluated through the multi-year Capital Improvement Plan, weighing infrastructure need against available funding sources (current revenue, grants, debt), with each project's timeline, cost estimate, location, and financing method documented. See [How Capital Projects Are Selected](intents/how-capital-projects-are-selected.md).

## A Note on Scope

This document intentionally does not take a position on whether any specific tax rate, spending level, project, or Board decision is right or wrong — that is a matter for the public process (elections, public hearings, Board votes) to resolve, not for this knowledge layer. Where this document references a statutory process (TRIM hearings, budget amendment procedures, referendum requirements for general obligation debt), that process exists specifically to give residents a formal, public opportunity to weigh in before a decision is finalized.

Where does Walton County get its money?

Walton County's revenue comes from local, state, and federal taxes, fees, licenses, grants, and payments for services. Walton County's revenue is organized into seven categories: General Government Taxes, Permits/Fees/Special Assessments, Intergovernmental Revenues, Charges for Services, Judgments/Fines/Forfeits, Miscellaneous Revenue, and Other Sources. # Intent: Why are my property taxes increasing?

## Question

Why are property taxes increasing (even when the millage rate stays the same)?

## Short Answer

Property tax bills can rise even when Walton County's millage rate doesn't change, because the tax is calculated by multiplying the millage rate by your property's taxable value — and taxable value can increase due to rising market values, new construction, or improvements, independent of any decision the Board of County Commissioners makes about the tax rate.

## Detailed Explanation

Ad valorem (property) tax is calculated as: **Millage Rate × Taxable Value ÷ 1,000**. One mill equals $1.00 of tax for every $1,000 of taxable value. The millage rate is set annually by the Board of County Commissioners through the budget process. Taxable value, however, is determined independently by the Property Appraiser under Florida law — it is the property's assessed value minus any exemptions (like the $25,000 homestead exemption).

If your property's market value rises and its assessed value rises with it, your taxable value increases even if the Board does not raise the millage rate at all — meaning your tax bill can increase purely from valuation changes the Board does not control. This is why "the County didn't raise the rate but my bill went up" is both common and consistent with how the system is designed to work.

## Why This Matters

This is one of the most frequent points of public confusion about local taxes. Understanding the distinction between the millage rate (a Board decision) and taxable value (a Property Appraiser determination under state law) clarifies who is responsible for what part of a tax bill change.

## Legal Context

Property valuation and exemptions are governed by Florida property tax law and administered by the Property Appraiser, a separately elected Constitutional Officer whose office is not controlled by the Board of County Commissioners. The Truth in Millage (TRIM) law requires notices and public hearings before a final millage rate is adopted, so residents can see and respond to proposed changes before they take effect.

## Budget Context

The Board adopts a final millage rate each year as part of the annual budget process (see Budget Process). The "rolled-back rate" is the millage rate that would generate the same property tax revenue as the prior year, adjusted for value changes — a benchmark often used to describe whether an adopted rate constitutes a tax increase in real terms.

## Frequently Asked Follow-Up Questions

**Who decides my property's value?** The Property Appraiser, an independently elected Constitutional Officer, not the Board of County Commissioners.

**What is the rolled-back rate?** The millage rate that would produce the same ad valorem revenue as the previous year, given the new taxable value — used as a reference point for whether a proposed rate is a real increase.

**Can I appeal my property's assessed value?** Yes, through the Property Appraiser's office and, if needed, the Value Adjustment Board process under Florida law — this site does not administer appeals.

**Does a higher millage rate always mean higher County revenue?** Generally yes, all else equal, but total ad valorem revenue depends on both the rate and the total taxable value across the county.

# Intent: Why can't tourist tax money pay for roads, schools, or other County needs?

## Question

Why can't the Tourist Development Tax pay for roads, general government, or other non-tourism County needs?

## Short Answer

The Tourist Development Tax is authorized under Florida Statute 125.0104 for a specific purpose — funding tourism-related projects and activities — and Walton County deposits it into the dedicated Tourist Development Fund. State law restricts how that fund's money can legally be spent, so it cannot be redirected to unrelated general government functions like road paving, courts, or general administration.

## Detailed Explanation

Florida's local option Tourist Development Tax (also called a "bed tax") is a tax on short-term lodging rentals (hotels, vacation rentals, etc.), paid by visitors rather than by county residents' property. It was established statewide by Florida Statute 125.0104 in 1977 specifically to give counties a mechanism to raise revenue for tourism promotion and tourism-related infrastructure — not as a general-purpose local tax.

Because the tax's authorizing statute defines what it can fund, Walton County accounts for it separately in the Tourist Development Fund, one of the County's major governmental funds. Money in that fund supports tourism promotion and marketing, beach renourishment and beach management, visitor-serving infrastructure, and tourism-related public safety initiatives (such as beach lifeguard services). It is not available to fund unrelated activities like general road resurfacing countywide, the courthouse, or general government administration, unless a specific project meets the statutory tourism-related nexus.

## Why This Matters

This is one of the most common points of public frustration: residents sometimes ask why a large tax collection "sitting" in a tourism fund can't just be used for an unrelated pressing need. The restriction is not a County policy choice — it is a state statutory requirement tied to the tax's legal basis.

## Legal Context

Florida Statute 125.0104 authorizes the tax and defines eligible uses, which generally include tourist advertising and promotion, tourism-related public facilities, beaches, and related infrastructure.

## Budget Context

Tourist Development Tax revenue flows into the Tourist Development Fund and funds departments such as Tourism Administration, Beach Operations, Beach Renourishment, Marketing, and Tourism Lifeguard Services.

**Who pays the Tourist Development Tax?** Visitors renting short-term lodging, not county residents' regular property tax bills.

**Can the Board decide to spend Tourist Development Tax revenue on something else if it wanted to?** Not without violating the statutory restriction on the tax's use — the restriction is a matter of state law, not just County policy preference.

**Does every Florida county have a Tourist Development Tax?** It is a local option available under state law; counties that levy it (like Walton County) must abide by the statutory use restrictions.

# Intent: How are roads funded in Walton County?

## Question

How does Walton County pay for road construction, maintenance, and drainage?

## Short Answer

Roads are primarily funded through the Transportation Fund, which is supported by motor fuel taxes, local option gas taxes, state revenue sharing, and grants — supplemented by transportation impact fees and mobility fees paid by new development, and by capital financing for larger projects.

## Detailed Explanation

The Transportation Fund is one of Walton County's six major governmental funds. It accounts for motor fuel taxes, local option gas taxes, state revenue sharing, and grants supporting roadway construction, maintenance, drainage, and infrastructure operations. These revenue sources are legally restricted to transportation purposes under Florida law — they cannot be redirected to fund unrelated general government services.

In addition to recurring fuel tax revenue, new development contributes toward the infrastructure its growth requires through impact fees and the Mobility Fee Fund, which fund the specific type of transportation infrastructure needed to serve new development, consistent with the "rational nexus" requirement under Florida impact fee law.

Larger road and drainage projects are typically included in the multi-year Capital Improvement Plan and may be funded through a combination of current revenues, grants, and, where appropriate, debt financing (such as revenue bonds secured by pledged transportation revenue) rather than paid entirely out of a single year's budget.

Day-to-day road maintenance, repair, and construction management are carried out primarily by the Public Works Department (maintenance, repair, construction) and the Engineering Department (capital project design and construction management, traffic operations, right-of-way permitting, and infrastructure grant administration).

## Why This Matters

Transportation funding restrictions explain why fuel tax revenue can't be redirected to, say, parks or general administration, and why new subdivisions are charged impact/mobility fees rather than the cost being spread entirely across existing taxpayers.

## Legal Context

Florida Statutes govern local option fuel taxes and state fuel tax revenue sharing formulas; impact fee and mobility fee programs must satisfy Florida's "rational nexus" test connecting the fee to the infrastructure burden the new development creates.

## Budget Context

See the Transportation Fund on Fund Financial Schedules, and the Transportation activity classification on the Summary of Expenses, which spans Public Works, Engineering, and transportation capital projects.

## Frequently Asked Follow-Up Questions

**Can gas tax revenue be used for anything other than roads?** No — it is legally restricted to transportation purposes.

**Do new subdivisions pay for the roads they need?** Largely yes, through transportation impact fees and the Mobility Fee Fund, calibrated to the infrastructure demand new development creates.

**How are major road projects like widenings or new roads funded?** Through the multi-year Capital Improvement Plan, typically combining current revenue, grants, and sometimes debt financing.

**Which department actually builds and maintains roads?** Public Works handles maintenance/repair/construction; Engineering handles capital project design, construction management, and permitting.

# Intent: Why does Walton County maintain reserves instead of spending all its revenue?

## Question

Why does the County keep money in reserves instead of spending it all or returning it to taxpayers?

## Short Answer

Walton County maintains reserves under its adopted Fund Balance Policy to ensure financial stability, cash-flow liquidity, emergency preparedness (including hurricane response), investment-grade credit ratings, and long-term fiscal sustainability — not because the money is unused or forgotten.

## Detailed Explanation

A government's "reserves" are formally called **fund balance** — the equity (assets minus liabilities) of a governmental fund. Walton County's Fund Balance Policy establishes fund balance classifications and reserve targets for specific reasons:

- **Cash-flow liquidity**: property tax revenue arrives in concentrated periods each year, but expenses are ongoing — reserves smooth that timing gap.
- **Emergency preparedness**: coastal Florida counties face hurricane risk; reserves fund immediate response and recovery costs before insurance, state, or federal reimbursement arrives (if it arrives at all).
- **Credit ratings**: rating agencies (Moody's, Standard & Poor's, Fitch) evaluate reserve adequacy as one factor in assigning a credit rating. Walton County holds an Aa1 rating from Moody's. A stronger rating lowers the interest rate the County pays when it borrows for capital projects, saving taxpayer money over the life of any debt.
- **Statutory contingency**: a specific "Reserve for Contingencies" is a budgetary reserve consistent with statutory authority, available to meet unexpected needs during the fiscal year.

Reserves are not simply idle cash — some fund balance is legally restricted or committed to a specific purpose and cannot be spent on general operations even if the Board wanted to.

## Why This Matters

"Why doesn't the County just spend the reserve" is one of the most common budget questions. The honest answer is that maintaining reserves at policy-driven levels is what allows the County to respond to emergencies without immediately raising taxes or fees, and what keeps its borrowing costs low on future infrastructure debt.

## Legal Context

Fund balance classifications generally follow governmental accounting standards; Walton County's specific reserve targets are set by Board-adopted policy, not by state mandate alone, though sound reserve practice is also a factor rating agencies weigh under Generally Accepted Accounting Principles reporting.

**Does every fund have the same reserve target?** No — reserve targets and fund balance classifications can differ by fund, based on that fund's own risk profile and policy.

# Intent: Why does Walton County government use debt/borrow money?

## Question

Why does Walton County borrow money (issue debt) instead of just paying for everything with current tax revenue?

## Short Answer

Walton County uses debt selectively to fund large, long-lived capital projects — spreading the cost of an asset (like major infrastructure) over the years residents actually benefit from it, rather than requiring one year's taxpayers to pay the entire cost upfront, while maintaining strict fiscal discipline that has earned the County an Aa1 credit rating from Moody's.

## Detailed Explanation

Walton County can issue several types of debt, each suited to a different purpose:

- **General obligation debt**, supported by property tax revenue, requires voter approval via bond referendum. The County retired its last outstanding general obligation bonded debt in 2010 and has had no special assessment bonded debt in the last ten years.
- **Revenue-backed debt** (revenue bonds, notes) is secured only by specific, dedicated, identified revenue sources — such as a pledged sales tax or utility fee — not by the County's general taxing power. If bondholders are not repaid, they have a legal claim only against the pledged revenue, not general County assets.
- **Non-ad valorem revenue covenants**, where the County commits to budget sufficient non-ad valorem revenue to service the debt, subject to first paying for essential government services.
- **Capital leases**, used to obtain long-term assets instead of purchasing them outright — sometimes costing more over the life of the lease than an outright purchase, but useful in specific financing circumstances.
- **State Revolving Fund loans and pooled/low-interest loan programs**, used for qualifying water, sewer, and flood control infrastructure projects.

As an example of dedicated revenue-backed debt, the County committed a portion of its future half-cent sales tax revenue to repay a $27,000,000 note issued in fiscal year 2015 and a $2,500,000 note issued in 2020, financing public improvements including a Broadband project; that debt is scheduled to fully mature in fiscal year 2030 and is repaid exclusively from the dedicated half-cent sales tax approved by the Board effective January 1, 2013 — not from general ad valorem tax revenue.

Neither the Florida Constitution, Florida Statute 200.181, nor Walton County itself has set a legal debt limit, but the County exercises fiscal discipline in assuming any debt, ensuring obligations are manageable and supported by reliable revenue.

## Why This Matters

Debt is often perceived negatively, but for large infrastructure investments it can be the fiscally responsible choice — spreading cost across the years of use, avoiding a large one-time tax spike, and, when structured with a dedicated revenue pledge, not placing general taxpayers at risk if a specific project underperforms.

## Legal Context

The Florida Constitution and Florida Statute 200.181 govern debt limits (Walton County has none formally set, but exercises fiscal discipline). General obligation debt requires voter referendum approval.

# Intent: How is Walton County's budget created?

## Question

How does Walton County create its annual budget, from start to finish?

## Short Answer

The budget moves through four phases — Preparation, Review, Workshops, and Adoption — beginning around February and ending in September with the Board of County Commissioners' legal adoption of a final millage rate and budget, as required by Florida Statutes Chapters 125, 129, 200, and 218.

## Detailed Explanation

**Phase 1 — Preparation**: departments receive instructions from the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) and prepare operating and capital requests based on service needs, staffing, projects, and funding assumptions, reviewing current programs and requirements.

**Phase 2 — Review**: OMB and County Administration evaluate department requests against revenue estimates, County priorities, fund capacity, and policy guidance, comparing proposed expenditures to available resources and preparing recommendations for the Board.

**Phase 3 — Workshops**: the tentative budget is reviewed publicly with the Board, including funding requests, service levels, and the proposed millage rate.

**Phase 4 — Adoption**: Florida law requires public hearings before the Board adopts the final millage rate and annual operating budget.

The budget is prepared on a basis consistent with Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) and the modified accrual basis for governmental funds, with encumbrance accounting used to track outstanding commitments and grant revenue generally added only after the underlying grant agreement is executed (or as prior-year grant balances carry forward). Budgetary control is established at the fund level, meaning total appropriations for each fund cannot legally exceed what the Board adopts, even as individual line items shift throughout the year.

After adoption, the budget remains an active management tool: OMB and departments monitor spending, revenue, and appropriations throughout the fiscal year, and the Board may amend the budget by resolution under Section 129.06, Florida Statutes, when unforeseen expenditures, contingency needs, unanticipated revenue, grants, or carry-forward funding require it.

## Why This Matters

Understanding the multi-month, multi-phase process — and that it culminates in legally required public hearings — clarifies both how much public input opportunity exists and why the budget can't be changed on short notice outside the formal amendment process.

## Legal Context

Chapters 125, 129, 200, and 218, Florida Statutes govern the county budget process broadly; Section 129.06 governs mid-year amendments; Florida's Truth in Millage (TRIM) law governs the public notice and hearing requirements before final millage adoption.

# Intent: How does growth affect Walton County's budget?

## Question

How does population and development growth affect Walton County's budget and spending?

## Short Answer

Growth increases the demand for infrastructure and services (roads, permitting, public safety, water/sewer capacity), which the County manages partly through growth-pays-for-growth mechanisms like impact fees and mobility fees, and partly through long-range planning tools like the Comprehensive Plan, Land Development Code, and concurrency management, which the Planning Department administers.

## Detailed Explanation

The Walton County Planning & Development Services Department provides professional land use planning advice to the Board of County Commissioners and is responsible for implementing, maintaining, and updating the Comprehensive Plan and Land Development Code, concurrency management (including implementation of the Mobility Plan), floodplain management, and development review in unincorporated Walton County — ensuring proposed development complies with adopted codes, policies, and regulations.

**Concurrency management** is the growth-management principle that public infrastructure and services (roads, water/sewer, schools, parks) must be available concurrent with the impacts of new development — meaning growth cannot outpace the infrastructure needed to support it. Impact fees and the Mobility Fee Fund operationalize part of this principle financially, requiring new development to fund the specific infrastructure its own growth demands (see How Impact Fees Work).

Beyond fee-funded infrastructure, growth increases demand across nearly every County service: more permitting and code compliance activity, more calls for law enforcement and emergency services, more demand on parks and libraries, and more pressure on water/wastewater systems like the Mossy Head Wastewater Treatment Facility. Some of this added service demand is funded through growth-linked fees; some is funded through general recurring revenue growth (since new development also adds to the property tax base over time) and requires ongoing budget planning rather than one-time fee revenue.

## Why This Matters

Understanding growth's dual effect — new revenue from impact fees and an expanded tax base, alongside new demand for services and infrastructure — clarifies why a growing county's budget both grows and requires careful long-range planning, rather than automatically becoming easier to fund.

## Legal Context

Florida's growth management framework requires local comprehensive plans, land development regulations, and concurrency management consistent with state statutes; impact fees must satisfy the "rational nexus" test under Florida impact fee law.

## Budget Context

# Intent: What do Walton County's Constitutional Officers do?

## Question

- **Clerk of Courts & County Comptroller**: supports judicial functions (Civil Division, Family Services, Felony Division, Jury Administration, Juvenile and Recording Divisions) and, as Clerk to the Board, provides Finance, Information Systems, and BCC recording services, including accounting/auditing, contract and purchase disbursements, investment of Board funds, and maintaining the County's Official Records.
- **Property Appraiser**: determines the value of all property in the County, maintains related records, calculates tax on taxable property once levied, and distributes Truth-in-Millage (TRIM) notices; its budget is approved by the Florida Department of Revenue, and the Board must pay municipalities' and the school board's share of its cost.

# Intent: How do impact fees work in Walton County?

## Question

What are impact fees, who pays them, and what can they fund?

## Short Answer

Impact fees are one-time charges paid by land developers at the time of development to cover the cost of constructing infrastructure (roads, parks, and similar facilities) needed to serve that new development — and Florida law requires a demonstrated "rational nexus" connecting the fee to the specific infrastructure burden the development creates, so impact fee revenue can only be spent on that matching category of infrastructure.

## Detailed Explanation

Impact fees are fees charged to land developers at the time of development for the cost of constructing facilities to serve the development site, such as roads and parks. Unlike a tax, an impact fee is directly tied to new growth: it exists specifically so that the infrastructure cost created by new development is paid by that development, rather than spread across all existing taxpayers who did not create the added demand.

Florida impact fee law requires a "rational nexus" — a clear, defensible connection — between the fee charged and the specific type of infrastructure it funds. This means a transportation impact fee can only fund transportation infrastructure, and a parks impact fee can only fund parks infrastructure; the categories cannot be commingled with each other or with unrelated general government spending.

In Walton County, related mechanisms include the Mobility Fee Fund (fees supporting multi-modal transportation infrastructure tied to new development) and the Recreation Plat Fee Fund (developer assessments supporting recreational facilities).

## Why This Matters

Understanding impact fees explains why a rapidly growing area may see new roads or parks built alongside development, funded by that development itself, while a resident's own property tax bill is not the source of that specific infrastructure spending.

## Legal Context

Florida impact fee law (developed through state statute and case law) requires the "rational nexus" test connecting a fee to the specific infrastructure need it funds, and generally requires fees to be proportionate to the burden new development creates.

## Budget Context

Impact fee and mobility fee revenue appears under Permits, Fees, and Special Assessments on the Summary of Revenues, and funds transportation and recreation capital projects in the relevant funds.

## Frequently Asked Follow-Up Questions

**Can impact fee revenue be used for a road project unrelated to new growth?** Generally no — it must fund infrastructure connected to the growth impact that generated the fee.

**Do existing residents pay impact fees?** No — impact fees are charged to developers at the time of new development, not to existing property owners on their regular tax bills.

**What's the difference between an impact fee and a special assessment (MSBU)?** An impact fee is tied to new development's infrastructure burden; a special assessment (MSBU) funds a specific local improvement benefiting a defined existing area, regardless of new growth.

**Does Walton County have a Mobility Fee in addition to traditional impact fees?** Yes — the Mobility Fee Fund supports multi-modal transportation infrastructure tied to new development.

# Intent: Why do budget amounts change from year to year (or from proposed to adopted)?

## Question

Why does a department's or fund's budget amount change from the prior year, or between the proposed and adopted budget?

## Short Answer

Budget amounts change for identifiable reasons: revenue performance, cost inflation on contracts and personnel, new state or federal mandates, one-time grants or capital projects entering or leaving the budget, Board policy decisions made during Workshops, and formal amendments adopted during the fiscal year under Section 129.06, Florida Statutes.

## Detailed Explanation

Several distinct, legitimate reasons cause budget figures to move year over year or within a single year's process:

- **Revenue performance**: if a revenue source like the Tourist Development Tax or sales surtax outperforms or underperforms projections, funding availability for the related fund changes accordingly.
- **Cost changes**: Personnel Services costs shift with negotiated/Board-approved compensation changes, health insurance premium changes, and state-mandated retirement (FRS) contribution rate changes; Operating Expenditures shift with inflation on utilities, fuel, contracts, and supplies.
- **One-time items**: a grant award, a capital project moving into an active construction phase, or a one-time asset sale can make a single year's figure much larger or smaller than a "typical" year, without indicating an ongoing trend.
- **New mandates**: new state or federal requirements (unfunded or partially funded mandates) can add cost obligations the County did not previously carry.
- **Board policy decisions**: during the Workshops phase of the annual budget process, the Board can add, reduce, or reprioritize funding requests based on public input and policy priorities.
- **Formal amendments**: after adoption, the Board may amend the budget by resolution under Section 129.06, Florida Statutes, to address unforeseen expenditures, use of contingency reserves, unanticipated revenue, new grants, or carry-forward funding.

Because Actual figures reflect what truly happened and Budget/Proposed figures reflect a plan made in advance, some year-over-year variance is also simply the normal, expected gap between a forecast and reality — not evidence that either figure was wrong.

## Why This Matters

A resident comparing this year's figure to last year's, or a proposed figure to what was ultimately adopted, needs to know these changes usually have a specific, explainable cause rather than being arbitrary.
