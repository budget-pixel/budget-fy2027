# Property Tax (Ad Valorem Tax)

## Purpose

This document explains how property (ad valorem) tax works in Walton County — how it's calculated, who determines each part of the calculation, and what it funds.

## Overview

Property tax and ad valorem tax are two names for the same tax — a tax levied on the assessed value (net of any exemptions) of real or personal property. It is the largest single revenue source for Walton County's General Fund.

## How the Tax Is Calculated

**Tax bill = Millage Rate × Taxable Value ÷ 1,000.**

- **Millage Rate**: a rate expressed in thousands — one mill equals $1.00 of tax for every $1,000 of taxable value. Set annually by the Board of County Commissioners through the budget process.
- **Assessed Valuation**: the value established by the Property Appraiser as the basis for levying taxes. Assessed valuation is required to approach 100% of market value in Florida; real property is appraised at time of construction or major remodeling and reappraised every two years.
- **Exemption**: amounts state law allows to be deducted from assessed value before taxes are calculated. A 1980 amendment to the Florida Constitution set the homestead exemption at $25,000; a 2008 amendment (Amendment 1) added an additional $25,000 exemption on homestead property values exceeding $50,000. Other exemptions apply to agricultural land, and to widows, blind persons, and permanently/totally disabled people meeting income criteria. Homeowners must apply for exemptions by March 1 of their first year of residence; renewal is automatic thereafter.
- **Taxable Valuation**: assessed value minus all applicable exemptions — the actual figure the millage rate is applied to.

## Who Sets Each Part

- The **Property Appraiser**, a separately elected Constitutional Officer, determines assessed and taxable value — independent of the Board of County Commissioners.
- The **Board of County Commissioners** sets the millage rate annually as part of the budget process.
- The **Tax Collector**, another separately elected Constitutional Officer, bills and collects the tax.

Because these are different authorities, a tax bill can change even when only one of the two factors (rate or value) changes — see Why Are My Property Taxes Increasing?

## Key Related Terms

- **Levy**: to impose taxes, special assessments, or service charges; also used as another term for millage rate.
- **Rolled-Back Rate**: the millage rate that would generate the same ad valorem revenue as the previous year, excluding new construction, annexation, or de-annexation. If a proposed millage rate exceeds the rolled-back rate, Florida law requires the public hearing notice to prominently disclose the tax increase and state it as a percentage above the rolled-back rate.
- **Aggregate Millage Rate**: the rate obtained by dividing the sum of all ad valorem taxes levied by the Board by the county's total taxable value — expressing an average tax rate across all County levies.
- **Final Millage**: the tax rate adopted at a taxing authority's final public budget hearing.
- **Adjusted Final Millage**: the actual rate levied when tax bills are issued, adjusted for corrected assessment errors or Value Adjustment Board changes — usually very close to the Final Millage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a mill of tax?** One mill equals $1.00 for each $1,000 of assessed property value. Example: a $250,000 home with a $50,000 homestead exemption and a millage rate of 3.6000 would owe $720 in county property tax.

**Is ad valorem tax the same as property tax?** Yes — there is no difference; they are different names for the same tax.

**Why is the County collecting more property tax if the millage rate didn't change?** Because taxable value (assessed value minus exemptions) can rise independently of the millage rate — see Why Are My Property Taxes Increasing?

**Can I appeal my assessed value?** Yes, through the Property Appraiser's office and, if needed, the Value Adjustment Board process — this site does not administer appeals.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The Board of County Commissioners decides how much my property is worth."** The Property Appraiser, an independent Constitutional Officer, determines property values — not the Board.
- **"A tax bill increase always means the Board raised taxes."** It can also mean the property's taxable value rose, with no rate change at all.
