# Intent: Why does Walton County government use debt/borrow money?

## Question

Why does Walton County borrow money (issue debt) instead of just paying for everything with current tax revenue?

## Short Answer

Walton County uses debt selectively to fund large, long-lived capital projects — spreading the cost of an asset (like major infrastructure) over the years residents actually benefit from it, rather than requiring one year's taxpayers to pay the entire cost upfront, while maintaining strict fiscal discipline that has earned the County an Aa1 credit rating from Moody's.

## Detailed Explanation

Walton County can issue several types of debt, each suited to a different purpose:

- **General obligation debt**, supported by property tax revenue, requires voter approval via bond referendum. The County retired its last outstanding general obligation bonded debt in 2010 and has had no special assessment bonded debt in the last ten years.
- **Revenue-backed debt** (revenue bonds, notes) is secured only by specific, dedicated, identified revenue sources — such as a pledged sales tax or utility fee — not by the County's general taxing power. If bondholders are not repaid, they have a legal claim only against the pledged revenue, not general County assets.
- **Non-ad valorem revenue covenants**, where the County commits to budget sufficient non-ad valorem revenue to service the debt, subject to first paying for essential government services.
- **Capital leases**, used to obtain long-term assets instead of purchasing them outright — sometimes costing more over the life of the lease than an outright purchase, but useful in specific financing circumstances.
- **State Revolving Fund loans and pooled/low-interest loan programs**, used for qualifying water, sewer, and flood control infrastructure projects.

As an example of dedicated revenue-backed debt, the County committed a portion of its future half-cent sales tax revenue to repay a $27,000,000 note issued in fiscal year 2015 and a $2,500,000 note issued in 2020, financing public improvements including a Broadband project; that debt is scheduled to fully mature in fiscal year 2030 and is repaid exclusively from the dedicated half-cent sales tax approved by the Board effective January 1, 2013 — not from general ad valorem tax revenue.

Neither the Florida Constitution, Florida Statute 200.181, nor Walton County itself has set a legal debt limit, but the County exercises fiscal discipline in assuming any debt, ensuring obligations are manageable and supported by reliable revenue.

## Why This Matters

Debt is often perceived negatively, but for large infrastructure investments it can be the fiscally responsible choice — spreading cost across the years of use, avoiding a large one-time tax spike, and, when structured with a dedicated revenue pledge, not placing general taxpayers at risk if a specific project underperforms.

## Legal Context

The Florida Constitution and Florida Statute 200.181 govern debt limits (Walton County has none formally set, but exercises fiscal discipline). General obligation debt requires voter referendum approval.

## Frequently Asked Follow-Up Questions

**Has Walton County ever had general obligation bonded debt?** It retired its last outstanding general obligation bonds in 2010 and has had no special assessment bonded debt in the last ten years.

**What secures the County's outstanding long-term notes payable?** A dedicated half-cent sales tax revenue stream, not general ad valorem tax revenue — meaning general taxpayers are not directly on the hook if that specific revenue underperforms (subject to the terms of the financing).

**What is Walton County's credit rating?** Aa1 from Moody's Investors Service, a strong investment-grade rating that reduces the County's borrowing costs.

**Is there a legal limit to how much Walton County can borrow?** No formal legal debt limit exists under the Florida Constitution, Florida Statute 200.181, or County policy, but the County applies its own fiscal discipline standards when assuming any debt.
