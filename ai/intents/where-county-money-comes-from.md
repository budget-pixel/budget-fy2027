# Intent: Where does Walton County's money come from?

## Question

Where does Walton County get its money?

## Short Answer

Walton County's revenue comes from local, state, and federal taxes, fees, licenses, grants, and payments for services. The largest sources are ad valorem (property) taxes, which primarily fund general government operations, and the Tourist Development Tax, which is legally restricted to tourism-related purposes.

## Detailed Explanation

Walton County's revenue is organized into seven categories: General Government Taxes, Permits/Fees/Special Assessments, Intergovernmental Revenues, Charges for Services, Judgments/Fines/Forfeits, Miscellaneous Revenue, and Other Sources. Each category funds different, often legally distinct, purposes:

- **Ad valorem (property) taxes** are levied on the assessed value of real and personal property, net of exemptions like the homestead exemption. They are the largest source of General Fund revenue and are largely unrestricted, supporting general government, public safety, and constitutional officer operations.
- **Tourist Development Tax**, established by Florida Statute 125.0104, is paid by visitors renting short-term lodging and is restricted to tourism promotion and related uses.
- **Fuel taxes** (Local Option Fuel Tax and state-shared fuel taxes) are restricted to transportation purposes.
- **Intergovernmental revenue** — state-shared revenues, federal and state grants — funds specific programs tied to each award's terms.
- **Charges for services, fees, and special assessments** fund the specific service or improvement they were charged for (e.g. an MSBU assessment for a paving project).

## Why This Matters

Understanding where money comes from explains what it can be spent on. A resident asking "why can't the County just use tourist tax money to fix my road" needs to understand that most County revenue is not one interchangeable pool — it is separated by fund, according to the legal restriction attached to its source.

## Legal Context

Florida Statutes Chapters 125, 129, 200, and 218 govern county revenue and budgeting broadly. Specific revenue sources have their own statutory basis — for example, Section 125.0104 for the Tourist Development Tax.

## Frequently Asked Follow-Up Questions

**How much comes from property taxes versus tourism?** See the live Summary of Revenues page for current totals by category — this varies year to year.

**Can the County create new revenue sources on its own?** Most new local revenue sources (new taxes, new fees) require specific state statutory authorization and often a local ordinance or referendum.

**Is all County revenue taxed the same way?** No — some revenue (ad valorem tax) is based on property value; other revenue (sales surtax, fuel tax) is transaction-based; still other revenue (fees, charges) is paid directly by service users.

## Key Takeaways

- County revenue comes from many distinct sources, each with different rules on what it can fund.
- Ad valorem taxes are the largest, most flexible revenue source, funding the General Fund.
- Tourism, fuel, and assessment-based revenues are legally restricted to their specific purposes.
- "Where the money comes from" directly determines "what the money can be spent on."

Walton County, Florida funds its government through a mix of local, state, and federal revenue: ad valorem (property) taxes, the Tourist Development Tax, fuel taxes, sales surtaxes, permits and fees, special assessments, intergovernmental grants and shared revenues, charges for services, fines, and miscellaneous income like interest earnings. Ad valorem tax — a tax on the assessed value of real and personal property, net of exemptions such as the homestead exemption — is the largest source of General Fund revenue and is the most flexible, supporting general government operations, public safety, and the county's constitutional officers (Clerk of Courts, Property Appraiser, Sheriff, Supervisor of Elections, Tax Collector). The Tourist Development Tax, authorized under Florida Statute 125.0104, is collected from short-term lodging rentals and is legally restricted to tourism promotion, beach management, and related visitor-impact purposes — it cannot fund unrelated general government functions. Fuel taxes are restricted to transportation infrastructure. Grants and intergovernmental revenue are tied to the specific program each award funds. Because Walton County uses fund accounting, each revenue source flows into a specific fund matched to its legal purpose rather than one shared, interchangeable pool of money — which is why a resident cannot simply redirect, say, tourist tax revenue toward a non-tourism-related expense.
