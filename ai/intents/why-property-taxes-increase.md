# Intent: Why are my property taxes increasing?

## Question

Why are property taxes increasing (even when the millage rate stays the same)?

## Short Answer

Property tax bills can rise even when Walton County's millage rate doesn't change, because the tax is calculated by multiplying the millage rate by your property's taxable value — and taxable value can increase due to rising market values, new construction, or improvements, independent of any decision the Board of County Commissioners makes about the tax rate.

## Detailed Explanation

Ad valorem (property) tax is calculated as: **Millage Rate × Taxable Value ÷ 1,000**. One mill equals $1.00 of tax for every $1,000 of taxable value. The millage rate is set annually by the Board of County Commissioners through the budget process. Taxable value, however, is determined independently by the Property Appraiser under Florida law — it is the property's assessed value minus any exemptions (like the $25,000 homestead exemption).

If your property's market value rises and its assessed value rises with it, your taxable value increases even if the Board does not raise the millage rate at all — meaning your tax bill can increase purely from valuation changes the Board does not control. This is why "the County didn't raise the rate but my bill went up" is both common and consistent with how the system is designed to work.

## Why This Matters

This is one of the most frequent points of public confusion about local taxes. Understanding the distinction between the millage rate (a Board decision) and taxable value (a Property Appraiser determination under state law) clarifies who is responsible for what part of a tax bill change.

## Legal Context

Property valuation and exemptions are governed by Florida property tax law and administered by the Property Appraiser, a separately elected Constitutional Officer whose office is not controlled by the Board of County Commissioners. The Truth in Millage (TRIM) law requires notices and public hearings before a final millage rate is adopted, so residents can see and respond to proposed changes before they take effect.

## Budget Context

The Board adopts a final millage rate each year as part of the annual budget process (see Budget Process). The "rolled-back rate" is the millage rate that would generate the same property tax revenue as the prior year, adjusted for value changes — a benchmark often used to describe whether an adopted rate constitutes a tax increase in real terms.

## Frequently Asked Follow-Up Questions

**Who decides my property's value?** The Property Appraiser, an independently elected Constitutional Officer, not the Board of County Commissioners.

**What is the rolled-back rate?** The millage rate that would produce the same ad valorem revenue as the previous year, given the new taxable value — used as a reference point for whether a proposed rate is a real increase.

**Can I appeal my property's assessed value?** Yes, through the Property Appraiser's office and, if needed, the Value Adjustment Board process under Florida law — this site does not administer appeals.

**Does a higher millage rate always mean higher County revenue?** Generally yes, all else equal, but total ad valorem revenue depends on both the rate and the total taxable value across the county.

## Key Takeaways

- A property tax bill = millage rate × taxable value ÷ 1,000.
- The Board of County Commissioners sets the millage rate; the Property Appraiser (an independent Constitutional Officer) sets taxable value.
- A constant millage rate does not guarantee a constant tax bill, because taxable value can change independently.
- Florida's TRIM law requires public notice and hearings before a final millage rate is adopted.
