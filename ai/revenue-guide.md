# Revenue Guide

## Purpose

This document explains each major revenue category Walton County collects: what it is, who pays it, why it exists, and what it can legally fund.

## Overview

Walton County's revenue is organized into seven categories on the Summary of Revenues (see [How to Read the Budget](how-to-read-the-budget.md)):

1. General Government Taxes
2. Permits, Fees, and Special Assessments
3. Intergovernmental Revenues
4. Charges for Services
5. Judgments, Fines and Forfeits
6. Miscellaneous Revenue
7. Other Sources

A critical fact for understanding the budget: **most County revenue is not interchangeable.** Many sources are legally restricted to specific purposes, and even revenue collected in the same broad category can be tied to different funds with different legal restrictions. See [Fund Structure](fund-structure.md).

## General Government Taxes

"Ad valorem, tourist development, and other locally levied taxes."

- **Ad Valorem Taxes (property taxes)**: a tax levied on the assessed value (net of exemptions) of real or personal property. The rate is expressed as a **millage rate** — one mill equals $1 of tax per $1,000 of taxable value. Ad valorem tax is the County's largest General Fund revenue source and is largely unrestricted, supporting general government operations, public safety, constitutional officers, and other countywide services. See [Property Tax](property-tax.md).
- **Tourist Development Tax**: established by Florida Statute 125.0104 in 1977, giving counties the option to raise additional revenue for tourism-related projects or activities. It is paid by visitors renting short-term lodging, not by county residents' property, and is legally restricted to tourism-related uses. See [Tourist Development Tax](tourist-development-tax.md).
- **Local Discretionary Sales Surtax**: a county-levied discretionary sales surtax authorized under Florida law.
- **Local Option Fuel Tax**: a locally levied tax on motor fuel, restricted to transportation purposes. See [Fuel Taxes](fuel-taxes.md).

## Permits, Fees, and Special Assessments

Revenue from permits, licenses, and special assessments — including building permits, beach activity permits, and Municipal Service Benefit Unit (MSBU) assessments. **Special Assessments** are compulsory levies against certain properties to defray the cost of specific improvements or services presumed to be of general benefit to the public and of special benefit to the assessed property (e.g. a paving or drainage project in a specific neighborhood).

## Intergovernmental Revenues

"Funds received from federal, state and other local government sources in the form of grants, shared revenues, and payments in lieu of taxes."

- **State Shared Revenues**: revenues collected by the State of Florida and proportionately shared with counties on the basis of specific formulas, including the Local Government Half-Cent Sales Tax, Mobile Home Licenses, Alcoholic Beverage Licenses, and Insurance Agents County Licenses.
- **Sales Tax (state-collected)**: taxes imposed on the sale of designated goods, rentals, and services under Section 212.054, Florida Statutes. These are collected and retained by the State, except for the Local Government Half-Cent Sales Tax Program, which returns funds to counties and cities by formula for any lawful purpose.
- **Federal and State Grants**: awarded for specific programs; grant revenue is generally added to the budget after the grant agreement is executed. See [Grants](grants.md).

## Charges for Services

Fees charged for specific County services rendered directly to residents and businesses — for example, ambulance fees, planning fees, recreation program fees, and golf course revenue at County-operated facilities.

## Judgments, Fines and Forfeits

Revenue from court judgments, fines, and forfeitures, including ordinance fines.

## Miscellaneous Revenue

Interest earnings, donations, and other revenue not classified elsewhere — including interest income on invested County funds, indirect administrative fee reimbursements between funds, and proceeds from the sale of surplus or fixed assets.

## Other Sources

Transfers in, debt proceeds, and other non-recurring funding sources — for example, a fund's beginning balance carried forward, or a transfer from another fund. See [Interfund Transfers](/pages/summary-of-interfund-transfers.html).

## Revenue Restrictions in Practice

| Revenue Source | Typical Restriction |
|---|---|
| Ad Valorem Taxes | Largely unrestricted (General Fund); some levies dedicated to specific funds (e.g. Sheriff Fund) |
| Tourist Development Tax | Tourism promotion, beach/shoreline maintenance, tourism-related public safety |
| Fuel Taxes | Transportation (roads, drainage, related infrastructure) |
| Impact Fees | The specific type of infrastructure the fee was charged for (e.g. transportation impact fees for roads) |
| MSBU/Special Assessments | The specific local improvement the assessment funds |
| E911 Fees | Emergency communications infrastructure and operations |
| Grants | The specific program or project named in the grant agreement |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Where does Walton County get most of its money?**
Ad valorem (property) taxes are the largest source of General Fund revenue; the Tourist Development Tax is a major source of tourism-restricted revenue. See the [Summary of Revenues (live page)](/pages/summary-of-revenues.html) for current-year totals by category.

**How much comes from tourism?**
Tourist Development Tax collections fund tourism promotion, beach management, and related uses through the Tourist Development Fund — see [Tourist Development Tax](tourist-development-tax.md) for the current total.

**Can tourist taxes pay for general government operations, like the courthouse or general administration?**
No. The Tourist Development Tax is restricted by Florida Statute 125.0104 to tourism-related purposes. See [Common Misconceptions](common-misconceptions.md).

**Why are grants counted as revenue instead of just a pass-through?**
A grant is real revenue received by the County, spent on the specific purpose the grant funds — it is budgeted as both a revenue (the grant award) and a corresponding expenditure (the funded program), which is standard governmental accounting practice.

**Why do some funds show revenue every year while others fluctuate wildly?**
Recurring revenue sources like property taxes are stable year to year; sources like grants, asset sale proceeds, or debt proceeds are one-time and can vary significantly by year depending on what was awarded or occurred that year.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Every dollar the County collects can be spent on anything."** Most revenue outside the General Fund's ad valorem tax base is legally restricted to a specific purpose. See [Fund Structure](fund-structure.md).
- **"Impact fees can be spent anywhere the County needs money."** Impact fees must fund the specific type of infrastructure they were charged for, tied to the impact of new development.
- **"Grants are free money with no obligations."** Grants come with specific program requirements, reporting obligations, and often a required local funding match — see [Grants](grants.md).
