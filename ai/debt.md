# Debt

## Purpose

This document explains why and how Walton County uses debt, and what its current debt profile looks like.

## Overview

Walton County uses debt selectively to fund large, long-lived capital projects, spreading repayment across the years residents benefit from the asset rather than requiring one year's taxpayers to cover the full cost upfront, while maintaining the fiscal discipline that has earned the County an Aa1 credit rating from Moody's Investors Service.

## Types of Debt Walton County Can Use

- **General obligation debt**: supported by property tax revenue; requires voter approval by bond referendum. The County retired its last outstanding general obligation bonded debt in 2010 and has had no special assessment bonded debt in the last ten years.
- **Revenue-backed debt**: secured only by specific, dedicated, identified revenue sources (e.g. a pledged sales tax, utility fee, or other non-ad valorem revenue) — explicitly not secured by the County's full faith and credit or general taxing power. If bondholders are not repaid, their legal claim is limited to the pledged revenue.
- **Non-ad valorem revenue covenants**: the County may covenant to budget sufficient non-ad valorem revenue to service debt, subject to first paying for essential government services; these bonds are not secured by a specific lien on that revenue.
- **Capital leases**: used to obtain long-term assets instead of purchasing outright; can cost more over the lease's life than an outright purchase, but suitable in specific financing circumstances.
- **State Revolving Fund and pooled loan programs**: low-interest loans for qualifying water, sewer, and flood control infrastructure projects.
- **HUD Section 108 loan guarantees**: a federal loan guarantee provision under the Community Development Block Grant program for economic development, housing rehabilitation, and large-scale physical development; Walton County currently has no outstanding HUD Section 108 loans.
- **Short-term variable rate debt / commercial paper**: cash management tools for capital expenditures that may later be refunded from grants or long-term debt.

## Current Debt Profile

The County has committed a portion of its future half-cent sales tax revenue to repay a $27,000,000 Note Payable issued in fiscal year 2015 and a $2,500,000 Revenue Promissory Note, Series 2020 — financing the planning, development, acquisition, construction, equipping, and maintenance of public improvements, most notably a Broadband project. Repayment relies exclusively on the half-cent sales tax approved by the Board, effective January 1, 2013 — not general ad valorem tax revenue. This debt is scheduled to fully mature in fiscal year 2030.

## Debt Limits and Credit Ratings

Neither the Florida Constitution, Florida Statute 200.181, nor Walton County itself has set a formal legal debt limit; the County exercises its own fiscal discipline whenever it assumes debt. Three nationally recognized rating agencies — Moody's Investors Service, Standard & Poor's Financial Services, and Fitch Ratings — provide independent credit-worthiness assessments that affect a bond's marketability and interest rate. Rating agencies weigh economic environment, debt history, administration, financial performance, and debt management, including evidence of sound practices like long-term financial/capital planning, expense growth kept in line with revenue, adequate operating reserves, GAAP-consistent financial reporting, and recognitions like the GFOA Certificate of Achievement and the Distinguished Budget Presentation Award. **Walton County holds an Aa1 rating from Moody's Investors Service.**

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Walton County have general obligation bonded debt outstanding?** No — its last general obligation bonds were retired in 2010, and it has had no special assessment bonded debt in the past ten years.

**What secures the County's current long-term notes payable?** A dedicated half-cent sales tax, not general property tax revenue.

**Is there a cap on how much Walton County can borrow?** No formal legal debt limit exists, but the County applies its own fiscal discipline standards.

**Why does a strong credit rating matter to taxpayers?** A higher rating (like the County's Aa1 from Moody's) lowers the interest rate on future borrowing, directly reducing the cost of financing capital projects.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The County is in debt like a household with credit card debt."** Government debt is typically issued deliberately for specific long-lived capital assets, often secured by a dedicated revenue stream rather than general taxing power, and is evaluated by independent credit rating agencies.
- **"Debt means the County is in financial trouble."** A strong credit rating (Aa1 from Moody's) indicates the opposite — sound financial management recognized by independent analysts.
